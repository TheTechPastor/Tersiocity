# **LOVE IN DEFENSE OF SMALL CHURCHES — Rewritten Edition**

  

### **Chapter 1 — The Sunday David and Sarah Came**


  

It was another ordinary Sunday morning at our little church—ordinary in the way small churches often are. I arrived early, unlocked the doors, turned on the heaters, checked the lights, and set up the projector and sound equipment. These small tasks had become familiar companions to my pastoral ministry, acts of preparation for the worship of God’s people.

  

Nothing felt unusual that morning until exactly 10:57 a.m., three minutes before the service began. That was when David walked in.

  

David was hard to miss—six-foot-one, three hundred pounds, and African American. That alone made his presence surprising in our tiny, aging, and almost entirely white congregation located fifteen miles south of Oakland. Our community had lived for decades in the long shadow of Oakland’s crime statistics. Fear had taken root in many hearts, even among believers. Few in our town ventured north unless necessity forced them.

  

So when David entered, our people noticed. And when he sat near the back with his girlfriend, Sarah, everyone wondered quietly how to respond.

  

Our congregation was composed mostly of widows with an average age of seventy-four. They had seen many seasons of life, many disappointments, and, sadly, many losses. But there was one thing they had learned well: how to show love.

  

After our first song and announcements, we entered into our customary greeting time—those warm minutes when we encourage hugs, handshakes, and genuine fellowship. I watched as one of our oldest widows—eighty-six years old and barely five-foot-four—shuffled determinedly toward David. Without hesitation she reached her arms around him as far as they could go. David stood frozen, unsure how to react.

  

Then another widow approached… and another… and another. One after another, these elderly saints wrapped David and Sarah in hugs and welcomed them warmly. The couple seemed stunned, unsure of what to do with such unguarded kindness.

  

Months later David told me that this was the **first time in his life** a white woman had touched him without fear or tension. He was stunned not only by their affection but by the fact that these women had left their purses on their chairs while hugging him. Trust and love were foreign experiences.

  

That morning broke something open in David and Sarah. Both had grown up in church—saved, baptized, and familiar with Christian language—but they confessed later that they had never _felt_ love like this. Not in all their years of church attendance. And it came from a group of elderly women who had no idea how unusual and powerful their simple Christian affection was.

  

As weeks passed and relationships grew, David and Sarah shared openly that they were living together. They came from a large church where divorce and broken relationships were normal. No one in their families had managed a marriage lasting twenty years, even among faithful churchgoers. In their minds, marriage seemed unnecessary—why enter something that looked doomed to fail?

  

Instead of gossip or condemnation, our prayer chain—those sweet, faithful intercessors—took them to heart. Not one unkind word was spoken. These older women prayed not for judgment but for redemption, asking God to guide this young couple into obedience and wholeness.

  

Love opened their hearts. Love softened their assumptions. Love made space for the Spirit to work.

  

Within months, David and Sarah witnessed something new to them: a couple in our church celebrating **sixty-five years of marriage**. That testimony moved them deeply. Before long they approached me privately and asked if I would marry them.

  

Four months after they first walked through our doors, our small congregation gave them a wedding overflowing with joy. Members from their former church came to watch, but it was our little fellowship—gray-haired, slow-moving, but overflowing with love—that stood and applauded when I pronounced them husband and wife. Many had tears in their eyes. They cheered not only because sin had been surrendered and righteousness embraced, but because David and Sarah had become family—the youngest in our congregation, loved as sons and daughters.

  

### **Why Did They Come?**

  

When I asked David later why they had chosen our church that particular morning, he smiled and said they “just felt like going to church,” and the car simply turned into our parking lot. After a year with us, he said he believed the Holy Spirit Himself had guided their car. I am not inclined to dismiss that.

  

For a year before David and Sarah arrived, I had been praying earnestly for someone—anyone—to help run the soundboard. Sundays had become a juggling act. I would set up microphones, adjust levels, preach, jump down to fix feedback, and then climb back up to continue my sermon. I prayed for relief. I even posted an ad on Craigslist. Several people responded, including one young man who wanted $15,000 a year to run sound on Sundays.

  

For a small, aging church barely surviving financially, that was impossible.

  

I kept praying.

  

When David and Sarah arrived, my focus was on welcoming them, not recruiting them. Yet a full year later, while praying again for help, David’s face came vividly to mind. The following Sunday I asked him if he would consider running the soundboard.

  

His reaction caught me off guard. His smile nearly swallowed his face, and he hugged me so tightly he almost lifted me off the ground. He explained that he and Sarah had grown up believing that every church member should serve—they just didn’t know how or where. When I invited him to serve, it felt like a gift.

  

He had never seen a soundboard before, but he learned quickly. And God used him beautifully.

  

With David faithfully taking care of sound, I was finally free to greet visitors, minister to people before the service, and be a pastor rather than a technician. Sarah soon offered to lead our monthly birthday celebrations—a ministry of joy and fellowship she carries with pride.

  

Were David and Sarah the “ideal” candidates for these roles? Not according to the world’s standards. But unquestionably, according to God’s.

  

### **A Lesson in God’s Shepherding**

  

Their story reminds me—and should remind all of us—that God cares more about hearts than about skill sets; more about community than perfection; more about obedience than polish. In small churches, God often sends the people we need—not the people we expect.

  

And the blessing is mutual. Our church loved David and Sarah into obedience; and David and Sarah, in turn, strengthened our church with their youthful joy and willing service.

  

In a world that worships size, programs, and performance, God still delights in using small congregations to accomplish eternal work—through love, faithfulness, and humble service.

## **Chapter 2 — And…**


  

When people hear the story of David and Sarah, some respond warmly—but others quietly wonder, _“Is it really that significant that two people started attending a small church?”_

  

To answer that question honestly, I have to take you back three years, to the very beginning of my pastoral journey at this congregation. Only then does the weight of their coming—and the story God was writing—become clear.

  

### **Returning Home to a Wounded Church**

  

I often tell people that this church raised me. I was saved here, baptized here, taught in Sunday School from kindergarten through high school, and nurtured in faith by men and women who walked faithfully with God. Like many young adults, I eventually left to pursue work and life elsewhere. Twenty-five years passed.

  

Then, unexpectedly, they called me back to serve as their pastor.

  

On my first Sunday in the pulpit, I saw a congregation deeply discouraged. Their average age was seventy-six. Their numbers had dwindled from three hundred to seventy. Ten years prior, the church had split dramatically—forty-five members walked out in a single business meeting and never returned. Their previous pastor had left abruptly, and the interim who stepped in had done little to lead or heal.

  

There was talk—serious talk—of closing the church, selling the property, and turning the keys over to the denomination.

  

They were tired, fearful, and unsure whether a future even existed for them.

  

### **Trying Everything the Experts Recommended**

  

Before becoming their pastor, I taught computer repair and web design at a community college. I had read widely on church growth, pastoral leadership, and ministry strategy ever since being called to ministry in 1984. I came into this role energetic, sincere, and convinced I knew “what works.”

  

So I started strong:

- door-to-door visitation
    
- direct mail campaigns
    
- ads in local newspapers
    
- yellow-pages listings
    
- every recommended “growth strategy” I could afford
    

  

Nothing worked. In fact, attendance declined.

  

Then the megachurch six miles away launched a massive outreach campaign advertising their dozens of programs, and our attendance fell from seventy to thirty-five in a matter of months.

  

We spent $10,000 on “growth efforts,” then ran out of money—and beyond that, we went into debt. There was no more room to advertise, no more budget to “attract,” no more energy in the people to fight discouragement.

  

That was when I stopped.

Not because I wanted to.

But because I had no choice.

  

### **When All Efforts Cease, God Begins to Speak**

  

With no money left and fewer people every week, I shut down every “growth program” and spent two months doing nothing but:

- praying
    
- visiting our current members
    
- listening to their hearts
    
- asking God for direction
    

  

I attended a pastors’ retreat during this time. When others asked, “What are you doing for growth?” I could only answer:

  

> “Nothing. I’m praying.”

  

I had never read a book or heard a conference speaker advocate _doing nothing_. Yet it seemed the only faithful thing I could do—because nothing I _had_ done bore fruit, and everything God wanted to do required me to get out of the way.

  

### **Discovering What God Actually Wanted**

  

Church-growth literature always ends with the same refrain:

“After we grew, we realized it was really God.”

Yet the same leaders then publish books and host conferences teaching _how they did it._

  

But in my small church—older, tired, and financially strained—the “growth formula” was impossible.

  

So I prayed.

And God answered.

  

After months of seeking Him, He impressed one clear, unmistakable calling on my heart—a calling that was at once simple, biblical, and utterly foreign to modern church-growth thinking:

  

### **“Care for these elderly saints. Love them well. Shepherd them into the arms of Jesus without fear.”**

  

That was it.

  

No programs.

No strategies.

No demographic targeting.

No marketing.

  

Just love.

  

I shared this calling with trusted members. Many told me it was sweet, compassionate… and, in their mind, completely impractical. _“That won’t grow a church,”_ they said.

  

Perhaps not.

But it would honor Christ.

And it would heal His people.

  

And so I began.

---

# **A Church Afraid—And Learning Not to Be**

  

The more I listened, the more I realized three illnesses had settled deeply in our congregation:

1. **Fear.** Fear of the future. Fear of dying. Fear of the church closing.
    
2. **Weariness.** Their bodies were aging; their energy limited.
    
3. **Loneliness.** They felt abandoned—by the split, by the previous pastor, by younger generations who no longer came.
    

  

So I began visiting faithfully—homes, retirement apartments, hospital rooms. I called, wrote cards, listened, prayed.

  

I asked them what they needed from me.

Their answer was simple:

  

> “Pastor, we just want someone to take care of us.”

  

So I did.

  

At the same time, I began a year-long sermon series on the **names of God**—twenty-six from the Old Testament and twenty-six from the New. Week after week, we studied:

- God’s faithfulness
    
- God’s sovereignty
    
- God’s protection
    
- God’s sufficiency
    
- God’s tenderness
    
- God’s love
    

  

And slowly, fear loosened its grip.

---

# **Teaching an Old Church to Love Again**

  

One major obstacle still remained: this was a congregation prone to gossip. I knew Scripture condemned gossip because it destroys love and unity. So I taught a Bible-study series on gossip and introduced one rule that I still enforce today:

  

### ****“If speaking about someone raises them in the eyes of the group, speak freely.**

  

If it lowers them, stop immediately.”**

  

The result was remarkable.

They didn’t stop talking about each other—they simply started telling stories that lifted one another up.

  

Love began to take root.

  

Week after week, I taught simple, practical ways love could be shown:

- write notes
    
- make phone calls
    
- take someone out for coffee
    
- visit during the week
    
- pray intentionally for one another
    
- celebrate small victories together
    

  

Some at first said, “Pastor, I don’t have time.”

But over the months, I began hearing stories—beautiful stories—of how they were living this out.

---

# **The Confirmation We Needed**

  

Visitors came from time to time—mostly relatives of our elderly members. Without exception, they told me:

  

> “I have never felt such love in a church.”

> “The moment I walked in, I felt it.”

> “This church is special.”

  

Those God-sent affirmations were our confirmation:

We were on the right path.

No programs.

No advertising.

No gimmicks.

  

Just love—real, embodied Christian love.

---

# **The First New Member**

  

The first new person to stay was a man who hadn’t been to church in thirteen years. On Super Bowl Sunday, on his way home from buying snacks for his party, he saw our church and decided to stop in—“for luck,” he said later.

  

He arrived late, left early, but filled out our visitor card.

  

That afternoon, I brought a plate of cookies to his front door. His home was filled with guests, food, noise, and a dog that nearly tackled me. I helped him catch the dog and handed him the cookies.

  

He was shocked.

He still talks about it.

  

He now attends faithfully whenever he is in town—and he is the Scripture-reader in our worship service. He is the professional DJ I once thought God had sent to solve our sound problem—only to discover God had other plans.

---

# **A Church Prepared to Love David and Sarah**

  

When David and Sarah eventually walked through our doors, the church had already been shaped by a year of intentional teaching and practice of love.

  

Their arrival looked sudden—but it was the fruit of a long season of preparation.

  

Because we had learned to love, we were ready to love them.

Because we had learned to trust God’s timing, we could receive them as a gift.

Because we had chosen faith over fear, we could embrace a man who looked “dangerous” to our elderly women—and they ran to hug him without hesitation.

  

The Holy Spirit had prepared the soil long before David and Sarah arrived.

  

That is why their coming mattered.

That is why it was significant.

That is why their story is not just a sweet moment, but a testimony to the power of love in a small church.

  

And that is why small churches matter deeply to God.

## **Chapter 3 — A Visit to Saddleback**


  

Several years ago, my wife and I drove to southern California to visit friends who lived in Santa Clarita—just across the freeway from Saddleback Church. Since we were so close, we decided to attend Saddleback the Sunday before Christmas.

  

Rick Warren wasn’t preaching that day; an associate pastor delivered the message. He listed “ten gifts” God supposedly wanted to give every person that Christmas. Most were drawn loosely—and sometimes inaccurately—from Old Testament prophetic passages meant for Israel, not modern believers. They were sincere statements, but not rooted in faithful interpretation.

  

The music, however, was impressive. Professional musicians filled the stage. A forty-piece orchestra accompanied choirs and soloists. The highlight was a stunning rendition of “Little Drummer Boy,” and the service concluded with a soaring operatic version of “O Holy Night.” It felt polished, practiced, and carefully produced.

  

When the service ended, the four of us went to a local taco stand. As we were eating outside, the speaker from that morning arrived with his family and sat about twenty feet away.

  

I like to encourage pastors whenever I can, so when we finished eating, I walked over, extended my hand, and said, “I enjoyed the service.”

  

He didn’t look up.

He didn’t smile.

He simply muttered, “OK,” and went back to his meal.

  

I stood there awkwardly, hand still half-extended, waiting a moment to give him the opportunity to respond differently. But nothing changed, so I returned to my table.

  

At first, I thought his behavior was rude. But as I reflected on it, I realized that what I had experienced was not rudeness—it was simply **normal** in a large-church, performance-centered model.

  

### **The Disconnect of Scale**

  

The speaker had no way of knowing whether I was:

- a member who had attended for years,
    
- a first-time visitor,
    
- or someone who hadn’t even been in the service.
    

  

He had no relationship with the crowd he addressed that morning. How could he? A preacher in a church that size doesn’t know who is present and who is absent. He doesn’t know who is hurting, who is rejoicing, who is drifting, or who is returning. He cannot.

  

Large churches operate like performance venues. The pastor becomes a host—much like a late-night television presenter—delivering content to an audience he does not personally know.

  

The show must go on, regardless of who is sitting in the seats.

  

If I had been part of that congregation for years, it wouldn’t matter. If I were brand new, it wouldn’t matter. If I never returned, it still wouldn’t matter. In a massive church ecosystem, there is no way for one man to shepherd that many souls personally.

  

And because there is no personal connection, there is little pastoral obligation to greet strangers in taco shops.

  

### **Why Small Churches Are Different**

  

In my congregation of thirty to forty people, a new face stands out immediately. If someone arrives early, I greet them before the service. I encourage them to fill out the visitor card, and—if they do—I personally deliver a plate of cookies or a plant to their home that afternoon.

  

If I see a member eating at a restaurant after church, I invite them to join my wife and me. We fellowship. We talk. We pray. We build relationship.

  

That is what shepherds do.

Not because the church is small, but because the **pastoral calling** is personal.

  

I teach these same people every Wednesday night. I visit them. I bury their loved ones. I sit with them when they are afraid. I encourage them when they are discouraged. I know them. They know me. There is transparency and accountability in both directions.

  

In a large church, this is impossible.

Not wrong—simply impossible.

  

### **The Stage vs. the Fold**

  

At Saddleback, there was no altar call, no invitation for prayer, no call for counseling, no space to meet with a pastor afterward. The message concluded with a reminder to “be courteous in the parking lot.”

  

There was no expectation of personal engagement.

The structure does not allow it.

  

When the primary gathering resembles a crowded auditorium, ministry becomes content distribution, not shepherding. The pastor becomes a communicator, not a caretaker.

  

Large churches often do amazing things—missions, events, relief projects, music programs, service projects, conferences. They have resources few small churches will ever possess. God uses them in real ways.

  

But “pastoring”—in the biblical sense—cannot be mass-produced.

  

### **Why This Matters for the Kingdom**

  

When I reflect on the difference, I don’t do so with criticism or resentment. Many leaders of large churches are godly, sincere, hardworking servants. Some of them are dear friends.

  

But the distinction must be recognized—and honored.

- **A large church can gather and instruct.**
    
- **A small church can shepherd and embrace.**
    

  

Both have value, but they are not interchangeable.

  

What concerns me is when churches begin competing instead of cooperating—when numbers become the idol, and pastors become CEOs instead of shepherds. When Christians seek entertainment rather than discipleship, and “programming” replaces fellowship.

  

In a small church, ministry is personal.

Love is personal.

Accountability is personal.

Discipleship is personal.

Grief is shared.

Joy is shared.

  

When I walked away from that taco stand moment, I didn’t feel wounded anymore. I felt clarity.

  

God was calling me to a different kind of ministry.

Not bigger.

Not flashier.

But deeper, slower, quieter, richer.

  

The ministry of a shepherd.

The ministry of presence.

The ministry that happens only in small churches.

## **Chapter 4 — Small**


  

When people ask what I do, I often tell them, “I’m the pastor of a small church.” I do this intentionally. Not apologetically—but honestly. Compared to the towering megachurches that dominate our cultural imagination, our little congregation of thirty or forty souls seems insignificant. But Scripture never measures significance the way America does.

  

Still, describing my church as “small” matters. It prepares people for a very different kind of ministry—one not built on performance, programs, or polish, but on love, presence, and faithfulness.

  

### **A Small Church in a Big-Church World**

  

My church is small partly because larger churches in the area actively draw people away from smaller congregations. On the first Sunday I preached as pastor, 89 people were in attendance. The next week, there were 60. After some research, I discovered that nearly all of those 29 visitors were regular attendees of two much larger churches nearby—Faith Fellowship and Neighborhood Church. They were not coming to join; they were coming to “check out the new guy.”

  

Faith Fellowship, just two blocks away, has around 850 people in worship and an exceptional music program. Neighborhood Church, six miles away, averages around 5,500 each weekend. Twenty people who had once attended our church and now belonged to Neighborhood came back for my first Sunday—but only for that Sunday.

  

I’m not against large churches existing—cities need many types of churches. But I do believe it is unhealthy and unbiblical when large churches compete for believers instead of laboring together for the lost.

  

### **Programs as Bait**

  

These larger churches invest heavily in music, drama, special ministries, and specialty groups—everything from financial coaching to support groups for parents of autistic children. When people call our church, their first questions are always the same:

- “Do you have a youth program?”
    
- “Do you have a recovery ministry?”
    
- “Do you have a singles group?”
    
- “Do you have a contemporary worship service?”
    

  

When I say no, the call ends quickly.

  

People have been trained to think of church as a department store designed to meet their felt needs. And in comparison, small churches look sparse.

  

Yet what they are missing is the one thing Scripture emphasizes far above programs: **love**.

  

### **A Different Kind of Ministry**

  

I once called Faith Fellowship and Neighborhood Church for help—asking to borrow a sound engineer or receive advice on strengthening our music program. Both declined. Neighborhood eventually put me in contact with someone who could help occasionally, but even that was sporadic.

  

Large churches, by necessity, focus inward to sustain their own machinery. Programs require staff, staff require budgets, budgets require attendance, and attendance requires attraction. The cycle is self-reinforcing.

  

Meanwhile, small churches survive by grace—literally. There are no “extra” people. No extra money. No staff. Everything depends on faith, prayer, and the daily obedience of ordinary saints.

  

And that, I have come to believe, is precisely why small churches matter.

  

### **The Unequal Competition**

  

Neighborhood Church is financially unique. Years ago, Caterpillar Tractor established an endowment for their founding pastor—providing them with enough funds to build their massive facility outright and cover their monthly operating costs. That means all money collected on Sundays goes directly into programs and outreach.

  

One of those outreaches is a phone-bank system that calls every number in Alameda County to invite people to church. They called my office once. The woman spoke very quickly, listing program after program without ever asking:

- “Are you connected to a church?”
    
- “Are you a Christian?”
    
- “Is there a way we can pray for you?”
    

  

It was pure marketing.

  

When I called back and spoke with the pastor, he denied that they were inviting members of other churches to leave. But the truth was obvious.

  

People from our congregation—17 men alone—left to join Neighborhood. Many of them told me directly that the programs at Neighborhood “met their needs better.”

  

Their departure devastated our men’s ministry. Our men’s Sunday School class dissolved, our men’s Bible study folded, and the monthly men’s breakfast ended.

  

Not because we lacked love.

But because we lacked programs.

  

### **The Heartbreak and the Turning Point**

  

In those early months, I was deeply upset:

- upset with the large churches siphoning away believers,
    
- upset with the people who left,
    
- upset with those who had returned for one Sunday just to “wish me well,”
    
- upset that no one warned me this would happen.
    

  

So I did what many pastors do when they feel threatened:

I launched an all-out effort to grow the church.

  

I called new residents.

Mailed flyers.

Ran ads.

Followed every church-growth formula.

  

The result?

- $13,000 spent
    
- 25 members lost
    
- and one pastor exhausted, discouraged, and utterly confused.
    

  

It was then—when I had nothing left—that God began gently tearing down my idols and replacing them with His vision.

  

### **“Teach them to love one another.”**

  

That was the whisper that began to reshape everything.

  

Not “create programs.”

Not “launch new ministries.”

Not “attract young families.”

Not “compete with the megachurches.”

  

Just this:

  

**Teach them to love.**

  

And so, Sunday after Sunday, as I preached through the names of God and names of Jesus, I taught about love.

  

Every week I said some version of:

  

> “If you want to show love to someone in this church, you could…”

> write them a note

> call them

> visit them

> bring coffee

> pray for them

> invite them to lunch

> find one small way to bless them

  

Over and over, for eighteen months, I taught the same simple truth.

  

The result?

Though 25 people left for Neighborhood, **eight brand new people came and stayed**—all drawn by the love in our fellowship.

  

A ninth person returned after leaving because she received so many cards, calls, and visits that she felt overwhelmed by how deeply she was missed.

  

### **What Research Misses**

  

Lyle Schaller, in _The Small Membership Church_, argues that both small and large churches are valid, but distinct, expressions of Christ’s body. Large churches help people “love God,” while small churches help people “love one another.”

  

Schaller is correct that small churches are not simply “miniature large churches.” Their DNA is different. Their strengths and weaknesses differ. Their calling differs.

  

But I cannot fully agree with his conclusion that the rise of large churches represents a shift in “religious agenda” toward loving God rather than loving people.

  

The New Testament never allows that division.

  

Jesus said:

  

> “By this all people will know you are my disciples—if you love one another.”

> —John 13:35

  

And in Matthew 25, Jesus described the final judgment not in terms of worship quality, doctrinal precision, or programmatic excellence, but in terms of **love expressed tangibly**:

- feeding the hungry
    
- giving drink to the thirsty
    
- welcoming the stranger
    
- clothing the naked
    
- visiting the sick
    
- caring for the prisoner
    

  

**These are personal acts**, not programmatic acts.

  

A megachurch can outsource them to staff or contractors.

A small church must perform them personally.

  

Which one more closely reflects Matthew 25?

  

### **What a Small Church Can Do That a Mega-Church Cannot**

  

In my congregation:

- I know every person by name.
    
- I know who is missing each Sunday.
    
- Missing people receive calls, cards, or visits.
    
- We sit together, eat together, laugh together, and bear each other’s burdens.
    
- We see each other’s tears, not just the backs of each other’s heads.
    
- We share life—not just a service.
    

  

A large church can produce breathtaking worship.

But a small church can produce breathtaking **love**.

  

A large church can create impressive programs.

But a small church can create deep **fellowship**.

  

A large church can execute flawless events.

But a small church can execute faithful **pastoring**.

  

These differences don’t make one inherently “better”—but they do make them profoundly **different**.

  

And in a world flooded with Christian entertainment, small churches remain the places where actual **Christian community** can still be lived.

**Chapter 5 — Feeding the Hungry**


  

As I continued meditating on Jesus’s words in Matthew 25—“I was hungry and you gave Me something to eat…”—I found myself thinking more deeply about what hunger looks like in our community. Hunger in America rarely resembles hunger in the ancient world. We don’t often see people dying of starvation in our neighborhoods. Social programs, charitable organizations, and emergency services usually prevent that level of suffering.

  

But Scripture does not say,

**“I was starving and you saved My life.”**

It simply says,

**“I was hungry.”**

  

Hunger takes many forms.

So does love.

  

**The Spirit’s Gentle Nudge**

  

One Wednesday afternoon, after praying through Matthew 25, I sensed the Holy Spirit nudging me toward something simple—but profound. I realized that the people attending our Wednesday night Bible study were, for the most part, elderly. Many of them came straight from doctor appointments, errands, or long afternoons alone.

  

Some had not eaten dinner.

Others ate poorly because cooking for one is lonely.

Several were living on fixed incomes and stretched every dollar.

  

And every single one of them was hungry—not starving, not destitute, but in need of care.

  

So I decided to feed them.

  

Not spiritually—though I did that too.

But physically.

Practically.

Lovingly.

  

After Bible study and prayer, I began cooking a full meal each week and serving it to everyone present—free of charge, no expectations, no donations, no conditions.

  

**A Table of Healing**

  

It’s hard to describe what happened next without emotion.

  

As we ate together each Wednesday evening, the room filled with:

• Laughter

• Conversation

• Stories

• Encouragement

• Prayer

• Tears

• Hugs

• Healing

  

Some talked about doctor visits.

Others shared griefs they had been carrying alone.

Some confessed fears they’d never voiced.

Others asked questions growing out of Sunday sermons.

  

In that simple act of cooking, the Spirit created a space where people opened their hearts and lives to one another. They ate together as the early church did—“with glad and sincere hearts.”

  

**What Megachurches Cannot Do**

  

A small church can gather around a single table.

A small church can feed every person in the room.

A small church can linger for hours and listen.

A small church can form family, not just fellowship.

  

A megachurch cannot do this—not for everyone.

Not personally.

Not intimately.

  

They can feed through programs or staff.

They can provide meals for segments of their congregation.

They can deliver food to the needy, donate to food banks, and support recovery centers.

  

Those are good things.

But they are not the same thing.

  

**Feeding is not a program—it is presence.**

**Hospitality is not an event—it is fellowship.**

**Love cannot be outsourced.**

  

What happened around those Wednesday-night tables was not “church programming.” It was the shepherding heart of Jesus made visible in casseroles, soups, salads, and conversations.

  

**A Meal as Ministry**

  

The Gospels reveal a Savior who frequently taught at tables:

• in the home of Levi

• in the home of Zacchaeus

• with Pharisees

• with sinners

• with His disciples

• with the 5,000 and the 4,000

• on the night He was betrayed

• and again on the shore of Galilee after His resurrection

  

Jesus fed people—both their souls and their stomachs.

He still does.

And He invites His shepherds to do the same.

  

In a small church, that invitation is not theoretical.

It is doable, weekly, tangible, and effective.

  

**The Real Point of Matthew 25**

  

The actions listed in Matthew 25—feeding, welcoming, clothing, visiting—are not a checklist for religious performance. They are the outflow of Christ’s love in His people.

  

The real question is not,

**“Which church has the biggest ministry?”**

but

**“Which people are most forming the heart of Christ within one another?”**

  

On Wednesday nights, as I cooked meals and served them, I was not building a program. I was shepherding souls. I was loving Christ in His people.

  

And I believe—I truly believe—that if Jesus Christ returned during one of those dinners, He would see His own heart reflected around those tables.

  

Not in the food.

Not in the room.

But in the faces.

In the love.

In the shared burdens.

In the joy.

In the laughter.

In the unity.

  

That is what small churches do best.

And that is what megachurches, by their very structure, cannot do for every person.

  

In small churches, love is not a slogan.

It is a meal.

A hug.

A visit.

A card.

A prayer.

A conversation.

A family.

  

Small churches are where Matthew 25 becomes flesh and blood—where Jesus is welcomed not on a screen or a stage, but in the person sitting two chairs away.

## **Chapter 6 — A Mission Statement of Healing**


  

Our mission as a church eventually came into focus with simple clarity. After years of striving, straining, and trying to apply the strategies of larger congregations, we discovered that God had already given us a mission perfectly suited for our size, our people, and our calling.

  

It reads:

  

### ****“To bring the people of San Lorenzo to a place of healing:**

  

spiritual, emotional, relational, and physical.

To supply people’s needs in a timely, appropriate, Christlike manner.

To provide a welcoming place of learning and healing where all may come and find Christ.

To receive, not repel, visitors—by being open, caring, and genuine.”**

  

This mission statement did not come from a conference, a workbook, or a denominational consultant. It came from prayer. It grew out of listening. It was formed through tears and deep reflection. It was shaped by souls who needed healing—and by a God who delights in bringing healing to the brokenhearted.

  

### **Healing as the Heart of Small-Church Ministry**

  

People everywhere carry wounds.

Some physical.

Many emotional.

Most spiritual.

  

We live in a culture saturated with anxiety, loneliness, and fear. Many people who walk through our church doors are exhausted—by their families, their finances, their bodies, or their memories. They aren’t looking for cutting-edge programming. They’re looking for rest.

  

A large church may have specialists, ministries, and teams designed to address various needs. But only a small church can offer something large churches cannot manufacture:

  

**presence.**

  

A listening pastor.

A caring congregation.

A family-like environment.

A church where everyone knows your name—and if you don’t show up, someone calls.

  

Healing grows best in environments where people are known.

  

### **Love at the Center of the Healing Process**

  

God placed love at the center of all true healing.

Not technique.

Not strategy.

Not structure.

  

Love.

  

As Dr. John Townsend beautifully writes, Jesus announced His mission with words of deep restoration:

  

> “The Spirit of the Lord is upon Me,

> because He has anointed Me

> to bring Good News to the poor.

> He has sent Me to proclaim that captives will be released,

> that the blind will see,

> that the oppressed will be set free.”

> —Luke 4:18

  

Jesus came to heal—to restore sight, break chains, mend hearts, and lift burdens.

  

If pastors are representatives of Christ, then the ministry of healing is not optional—it is central. And the small church, by its very nature, is uniquely suited to be a healing community.

  

### **The Tragedy of the CEO Pastor**

  

I have come to believe that a pastor is not truly a pastor if:

- it takes three weeks to get an appointment,
    
- a person can attend for years without ever meeting the pastor,
    
- the organizational structure resembles a corporation instead of a flock.
    

  

In large congregations, pastors often become administrators, executives, strategists, or figureheads. Many are sincere, gifted, and deeply committed to God—but the structure forces them into roles other than “shepherd.”

  

A bishop can lead thousands.

A CEO can manage budgets.

A president can coordinate staff.

  

But only a **pastor** can shepherd souls.

  

Gordon MacDonald once said:

  

> “Let’s save the title _pastor_ for the man or woman

> who goes face-to-face with people,

> whatever their need.”

  

I agree wholeheartedly.

  

Large churches may be led by godly CEOs, bishops, and presidents—but the shepherding heart of Jesus can only be carried out by someone who actually touches lives personally.

  

### **Money vs. Ministry**

  

I once attended a massive church-growth seminar led by John Maxwell’s organization. Speaker after speaker shared how they had reached 1,000 members. When pressed for details, none of them truly knew how it happened. Their stories were more miraculous than reproducible.

  

The seminar ended with a speaker declaring how “exciting” it was to manage a million-dollar budget. The clear implication was that the purpose of growth was money—and the purpose of money was more programs—and the purpose of programs was more growth.

  

People equaled revenue.

Revenue equaled expansion.

Expansion equaled success.

  

It became painfully clear that shepherding was not the focus. Feeding sheep was not the focus. Loving people was not the focus.

  

But feeding sheep is exactly what Jesus commanded Peter to do.

  

**“Feed My sheep.”**

  

Not expand My enterprise.

Not increase My brand.

Not scale My operations.

  

Just feed them.

  

### **The Joy and Cost of Small-Church Faithfulness**

  

In my own church, money has always been limited. Twice during my second year, there wasn’t enough to pay my salary. Other pastors advised me:

- “Get a promissory note.”
    
- “Demand back pay.”
    
- “Leave—they can’t support a pastor.”
    

  

But I forgave the debt.

I released the church from the obligation.

And I trusted God.

  

If my church grew to 1,000 people, I would likely enjoy a comfortable salary. But I would also lose what I cherish most—the ability to know, love, and care for everyone personally.

  

I can stand in the pulpit and say, “I love you all,” and mean it—not in a general sense, but individually. I know their stories, their families, their illnesses, their fears. I know their spiritual struggles and physical limitations.

  

I can visit them when they’re sick.

I can cook for them when they’re hungry.

I can pray for them by name.

  

That is the privilege of pastoring a small church.

It is holy ground.

  

### **God’s Provision Through Strangers**

  

Our church survives financially through unexpected means. Located between two Catholic parishes with large Spanish-speaking congregations, we discovered that families needed a place for gatherings—baptisms, quinceañeras, and celebrations the Catholic buildings could not host.

  

We offered our social hall at reasonable cost. They were strangers. Some spoke very little English. But we welcomed them—and through their celebrations, God provided enough income to keep the lights on and the doors open.

  

This, too, is love.

This, too, is hospitality.

This, too, is ministry.

  

### **Small, Slow, and Sacred**

  

I am not, nor will I ever be, a contender for the “Fastest Growing Church in America.” I am not a megachurch pastor. I am not a celebrity speaker. I am simply a shepherd of a small flock.

  

But I believe with all my heart that small churches—and the pastors who love them—are precious to Jesus.

  

As Mike Yaconelli liked to say,

**“I pastor the world’s slowest-growing church.”**

  

And that may be closer to the heart of God than we realize.

## **Chapter 7 — When Programs Replace People**


  

Every month our church receives phone calls from people looking for a congregation to attend. I can almost predict the first question they will ask. It is never about doctrine, preaching, community, or spiritual care.

  

Instead, they ask:

- “Do you have a youth program?”
    
- “Do you have a recovery ministry?”
    
- “Do you have a singles group?”
    
- “Do you have a strong children’s program?”
    
- “Do you have a contemporary service?”
    

  

The calls are polite, but their purpose is transactional. They are searching not for a _church_ but for a _service provider_. A kind of spiritual department store.

  

And as soon as they hear that we do not have the program they seek, the conversation ends.

  

Before I can say, “You’re welcome to join us for worship,” the dial tone is already humming.

  

### **When Church Becomes a Marketplace**

  

This way of thinking reveals something tragic:

Many Christians have learned to evaluate churches primarily by their **programs**, not by their **people**.

  

But discipleship is not a program.

Fellowship is not a program.

Love is not a program.

Shepherding is not a program.

Prayer is not a program.

Community is not a program.

  

These things are practiced—not provided.

They are lived—not packaged.

They are experienced—not advertised.

  

A church can have 450 programs and still lack love.

A church can have no programs and overflow with the Spirit’s life.

  

The New Testament never says,

“By this all men will know you are My disciples—by your excellent programming.”

  

It says,

“By your love for one another.”

—John 13:35

  

### **The Constraint of Small Churches**

  

Our church, like many small congregations, simply does not have the manpower or resources for a long list of ministries. With an older congregation—even one full of faith and willingness—our people do not possess the energy to run multiple weekly programs.

  

In a church of forty, if someone is absent, an entire ministry collapses. A small church cannot sustain fifty specialized subgroups. The need is greater than the workers available.

  

And yet, this limitation is also a gift.

  

Because when a church cannot rely on programs, it is forced—beautifully—to rely on **people**.

  

### **The Allure of Specialty Groups**

  

Large churches excel at niche ministries—support groups for:

- divorced parents
    
- grieving spouses
    
- parents of autistic children
    
- recovering addicts
    
- young adults
    
- professionals
    
- seniors
    
- singles
    
- men
    
- women
    
- teens
    
- pre-teens
    
- new believers
    

  

And the list goes on.

  

These ministries can be helpful. They serve real needs. They can bring comfort and guidance.

  

But they also create a subtle temptation:

people begin _choosing churches_ the way they choose gyms or community centers.

  

The question becomes not,

“Where is God calling me?”

but

“What can you offer me?”

  

This shift—from calling to consumption—hurts the church universal.

  

### **A Personal Example of Program-Driven Departure**

  

I once had a faithful leader—a deacon, a teacher, a man deeply invested in our congregation. He taught a class of autistic children at a local school. When he learned that Neighborhood Church had a support group specifically for parents of autistic children, he left our church abruptly.

  

His own children were not autistic.

He was not seeking help for his family.

He simply wanted the education and community of that specialty ministry.

  

He didn’t tell me.

I only learned months later through his wife.

  

During those months, she had received over twenty-five cards and more than fifty phone calls from our people—telling them they were missed, that they were loved, and that they still had a home at Cornerstone.

  

This is small-church love.

Personal. Persistent. Genuine.

  

Yet even genuine love can be overshadowed by the allure of programs.

  

### **What Nobody Asks**

  

In all these phone calls from seekers, nobody asks me:

- “Do you preach Scripture faithfully?”
    
- “Do your people love one another?”
    
- “Will I be known here?”
    
- “Is Christ exalted in your worship?”
    
- “Is this a praying church?”
    
- “Is this a place where my soul can grow?”
    

  

Not once.

  

Instead, the focus is always on amenities.

  

But church is not a vendor.

Church is not a dispenser of spiritual goods.

Church is not a menu to order from.

  

Church is a **body**.

Church is a **family**.

Church is a **flock**.

Church is a **bride**.

Church is a **temple** of living stones.

Church is a **community of saints** bound together by covenant love.

  

### **Program-Heavy Churches vs. People-Heavy Churches**

  

A church that relies on programs will always attract people who want programs.

  

But a church that relies on people will attract people who want **church**.

  

Small churches are rarely chosen for their amenities.

They are chosen for their authenticity.

  

For presence.

For love.

For simplicity.

For nearness.

For relationship.

For Christlikeness.

  

In small churches, fellowship is not manufactured—it happens naturally.

In small churches, needs are not addressed by departments—they are addressed by friends.

In small churches, love is not scheduled—it is lived.

  

### **A Faithful Remnant in a Program-Driven Culture**

  

There will always be people who prefer the anonymity of a crowd, the spectacle of a stage, or the schedule of specialty groups. That is their choice.

  

But there will also always be a remnant—those whom God draws to quiet places, to simple worship, to deep fellowship, to shepherding relationships, to churches where they are known, missed, and loved.

  

Small churches are not for everyone.

But they are essential for the body of Christ.

  

Because small churches remind us that the Kingdom of God is not built on marketing strategies, production value, or programmatic sophistication.

  

The Kingdom is built on:

- faith
    
- prayer
    
- presence
    
- sacrifice
    
- community
    
- love
    
- shepherding
    
- obedience
    
- and the power of the Holy Spirit
    

  

These things cannot be purchased.

They cannot be scheduled.

They cannot be programmed.

  

They can only be lived.

## **Chapter 8 — The Power of Small-Church Love**


  

About six months before writing this chapter, a new family walked through the doors of our church: a mother named JoAnn and her three daughters—Sally (15), Tina (14), and little Andrea (4). They were Vietnamese, and their presence was a reminder that the Kingdom of God is gloriously diverse. Christ draws people “from every tribe and language and people and nation,” and a loving church will attract exactly that.

  

It was my fourth anniversary Sunday. We had planned a celebration after the service, complete with a time of sharing. This family slipped in quietly and found a seat.

  

What brought them that day was a question from a four-year-old. The day before, Andrea had asked her mother how to pray. JoAnn realized she had no answer—because they had not been in church since Andrea was born. She grabbed the phone book, looked for a church, liked our name, and came.

  

Sometimes the Holy Spirit leads through the simple honesty of a child.

  

### **Love Recognized Instantly**

  

After only an hour among us, JoAnn stood during the sharing time and said:

  

> “We feel like we belong.

> Even though we’ve been here one hour,

> we feel like family.”

  

Those words touched every heart in the room. They were not praising our programs, our music, or our facilities. They were describing what Jesus said would identify His followers: **love**.

  

They have attended ever since—except when knocked down by the flu.

  

### **A Contrast Worth Noting**

  

I know a couple who attends Jubilee, a massive megachurch in Sunnyvale. Their church experience is the polar opposite of JoAnn’s family. When we meet with them socially and ask how church is, they tell us stories about things that happened **on the stage**:

- A guest performer
    
- A soloist
    
- A professional choir
    
- A dramatic presentation
    
- An impressive piece of music
    

  

Once they told us about a guest who had memorized large portions of Scripture. But when we ask what the sermon was about, they can never recall the message. And they never know who they sat next to. With seven weekend services and thousands of attendees, it is impossible.

  

Their eyes are on the platform—not the people.

  

And tragically, in many large churches, the platform becomes the center of spiritual life.

  

### **The Stage and the Savior**

  

Jubilee’s sanctuary has a massive curtain painted with a dramatic image of Jesus returning on a white horse, the armies of heaven behind Him. And on the roof of the sanctuary building, the word “JUBILEE” is written in massive letters—so that, symbolically, Jesus will know where to land when He returns.

  

It is impressive.

It is grand.

It is artistic.

But it is not the heart of Christianity.

  

The heart of Christianity is not found on a stage—it is found in the people.

  

Jesus said:

  

> “Where two or three are gathered in My name,

> there I am in the midst of them.”

> —Matthew 18:20

  

He did not say:

  

> “Where thousands gather with lasers, lights, and orchestras…”

  

He said **two or three**.

  

Jesus Himself was born in obscurity, ministered in small towns, and chose disciples from humble places. He touched lepers, talked with outcasts, sat at small tables, and stayed in ordinary homes.

  

This is why small churches matter:

They reflect the actual ministry of Jesus in ways large churches simply cannot.

  

### **The Hard Part: Love Becomes Personal**

  

The command “love one another” becomes much harder—and much more real—in a small church.

  

Because in a small church:

- You know everyone.
    
- Everyone knows you.
    
- You cannot hide in a crowd.
    
- You cannot avoid difficult people for long.
    
- You cannot pick and choose who to love.
    
- Your absence is noticed.
    
- Your presence matters.
    

  

Bruce Larson once wrote:

  

> “We tend to love the lovable…

> but we struggle with the people who sit in our spot,

> who annoy us,

> who have hurt us,

> or who seem phony or difficult.”

  

In a large church, avoiding such people is easy. If someone bothers you, you simply switch services, sit in a different section, or slip in late and leave early.

  

But in a small church, love becomes obedience, not preference.

  

Everyone is your brother or sister.

Everyone is part of the flock.

Everyone becomes a responsibility.

  

This is uncomfortable—until you embrace it.

And when you do, it becomes transforming.

  

### **An Atmosphere Thick With Love**

  

A pastor who visited our church one Sunday told me afterward:

  

> “The love in this place is so thick

> you could cut it with a knife.”

  

He said he had served a church for eighteen years and had never experienced a single hour like the one he spent with us.

  

He asked me to pray that the new church he was planting would experience even a fraction of the love he felt at Cornerstone.

  

This is the power of small-church love.

It is tangible.

It is memorable.

It is supernatural.

It is Christlike.

  

### **Programs Impress—Love Changes Lives**

  

Larger churches can impress.

Small churches can embrace.

  

Larger churches can inspire crowds.

Small churches can restore souls.

  

Larger churches can offer spectacles.

Small churches can offer family.

  

Which one resembles the Kingdom Jesus described?

He spoke of:

- shepherds
    
- flocks
    
- little children
    
- small gatherings
    
- lost sheep
    
- mustard seeds
    
- quiet prayer
    
- washing feet
    
- breaking bread in homes
    

  

These are small-scale images.

Personal.

Relational.

Human.

Intimate.

Loving.

  

### **A Church That Loves Like Jesus**

  

A church of forty people can live out every “one another” in Scripture:

- love one another
    
- forgive one another
    
- bear one another’s burdens
    
- pray for one another
    
- confess sins to one another
    
- greet one another
    
- encourage one another
    
- serve one another
    

  

Large churches teach these commands.

Small churches practice them.

  

That is not criticism—it is reality.

  

And it is a reality that demonstrates why small churches are precious to Christ.

## **Conclusion — In Praise of the Faithful, the Small, and the Loved**

  

This book began with a simple story—a young couple walking into a small aging church and discovering, to their surprise, a love they had never known. Their experience was not an anomaly. It was a window into the heart of what small churches are uniquely gifted to give: **love, presence, and the ministry of Christlike healing.**

  

Throughout these chapters we have explored the realities facing small churches: the pressures of the megachurch era, the dominance of programs, the consumer mindset shaping modern Christianity, and the discouragement many pastors feel as they watch their congregations age, shrink, or struggle. Yet woven through every story is a deeper truth:

  

### **Small churches are not failing churches.**

  

### **Small churches are faithful churches.**

  

### **Small churches are biblical churches.**

  

### **Small churches are beloved by Jesus.**

  

The world measures success by size, budgets, staff, and visibility. But Christ measures His church by love, obedience, faithfulness, and the depth of care His people show one another.

  

### **What Small Churches Do Best**

  

A small church cannot offer endless programs, polished productions, or stage-driven worship experiences. But it can offer something far more precious:

- **A pastor who knows every person by name**
    
- **A family where absence is noticed and presence is valued**
    
- **A community where love is practiced, not advertised**
    
- **A fellowship where every burden is shared**
    
- **A sanctuary where wounded souls can heal slowly and safely**
    
- **A place where the lonely become known, and the hurting become held**
    

  

In small churches, the commands of Scripture become real:

- “Love one another.”
    
- “Bear one another’s burdens.”
    
- “Encourage one another.”
    
- “Forgive one another.”
    
- “Pray for one another.”
    
- “Confess your sins to one another.”
    

  

These commands cannot be mass-produced.

They cannot be industrialized.

They cannot be outsourced.

  

They require **relationship**.

They require **time**.

They require **presence**.

They require **love**.

  

And small churches excel at love.

  

### **A Needed Corrective to the Modern Church**

  

In an era increasingly captivated by size, spectacle, and celebrity, small churches quietly offer a corrective witness—one rooted not in innovation but in Scripture itself.

  

Jesus spent much of His earthly ministry in small villages, homes, and gatherings. He discipled twelve. He sat at small tables. He walked dusty paths. He embraced children. He touched the forgotten. He spoke to individuals by name.

  

Christianity began not in stadiums, but in living rooms.

Not in megachurches, but in households.

Not through programs, but through people.

Not through marketing, but through love.

  

Small churches preserve this ancient rhythm.

  

### **A Hopeful Future**

  

There is a future for the small church—a bright one. As the world becomes more impersonal, more anxious, more fractured, people hunger for what small congregations naturally embody:

- Authentic community
    
- Genuine fellowship
    
- Slow discipleship
    
- Deep relationships
    
- Pastoral presence
    
- Trust
    
- Family
    
- Home
    

  

The future will belong not to the church that shouts the loudest, but to the church that **loves the deepest**.

  

### **A Final Word to the Pastor of the Small Church**

  

If you are a pastor reading this:

  

Take heart.

Your work matters.

Your church matters.

Your people matter.

Your love matters.

Your faithfulness is seen by God.

  

You are not “less than.”

You are not “behind.”

You are not “failing.”

  

You are shepherding God’s flock.

You are modeling the heart of Jesus.

You are building something eternal.

  

Large churches may gather crowds.

But small churches grow disciples.

Large churches may impress.

But small churches embrace.

Large churches may influence.

But small churches transform.

  

Do not despise the day of small things.

God never has.

He never will.

  

### **A Final Word to the Member of the Small Church**

  

You belong to something rare and beautiful. The love you give and the love you receive is a testimony to the world. You are living proof of John 13:35—that the world will know we are disciples of Jesus by our love for one another.

  

Cherish your church.

Encourage your pastor.

Love your people.

Stay faithful.

Stay united.

Stay joyful.

Stay small if God calls you to—but stay committed to love no matter what.

  

Because in the end, this is the true defense of the small church:

  

### **Small churches show the world the heart of Christ—one person at a time.**

  

And that is a work heaven celebrates.
