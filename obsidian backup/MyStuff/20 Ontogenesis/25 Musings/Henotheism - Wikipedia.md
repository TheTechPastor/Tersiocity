From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Worship of a single god while not denying the existence or possible existence of other deities

**Henotheism** is the worship of a single, supreme [god](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/God "God") that does not deny the existence or possible existence of other [deities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deities "Deities") that may be worshipped.[^noll2001-1][^taliaferro78-2][^britannicamonolatry-3] [Friedrich Schelling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Friedrich_Schelling "Friedrich Schelling") (1775–1854) coined the word, and [Friedrich Welcker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Friedrich_Welcker "Friedrich Welcker") (1784–1868) used it to depict primitive [monotheism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monotheism "Monotheism") among ancient Greeks.[^4]

[Max Müller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Max_M%C3%BCller "Max Müller") (1823–1900), a British philologist and [orientalist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orientalism "Orientalism"), brought the term into wider usage in his scholarship on the [Indian religions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_religions "Indian religions"),[^5][^alonp370-6] particularly [Hinduism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hinduism "Hinduism") whose scriptures mention and praise numerous deities as if they are one ultimate unitary divine essence.[^taliaferro78-2] Müller made the term central to his criticism of [Western](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_world "Western world") theological and religious [exceptionalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exceptionalism "Exceptionalism") (relative to [Eastern religions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_religion "Eastern religion")), focusing on a cultural [dogma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dogma "Dogma") which held "monotheism" to be both fundamentally well-defined and inherently superior to differing [concepts of God](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Concepts_of_God "Concepts of God").[^7]

## Definition and terminology

Friedrich Schelling coined the German term *Henotheismus* from [Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_language "Greek language") ἕν *(hén)* 'one' and German *Theismus* 'theism' (which comes from [Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_language "Greek language") θεός *(theós)* 'god').[^taliaferro78-2][^8][^fahlbusch524-9] The term refers to a form of [theism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theism "Theism") focused on a single god. Related terms are [monolatry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monolatry "Monolatry") and [kathenotheism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kathenotheism "Kathenotheism").[^britannicamonolatry-3] The latter term is an extension of "henotheism", from καθ' ἕνα θεόν *(kath' hena theon)* '"one god at a time"'.[^10] Henotheism refers to a pluralistic theology wherein different deities are viewed to be of a unitary, equivalent divine essence.[^taliaferro78-2] Another term related to henotheism is "equitheism", referring to the belief that all gods are equal.[^11] Furthermore, the term henotheism does not exclude [monism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monism "Monism"), [nondualism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nonduality_\(spirituality\) "Nonduality (spirituality)"), or [dualism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dualistic_cosmology "Dualistic cosmology").[^alonp370-6]

Various scholars prefer the term monolatry to henotheism, to discuss religions where a single god is central, but the existence or the position of other gods is not denied.[^britannicamonolatry-3][^fahlbusch524-9] According to Christoph Elsas, henotheism in modern usage connotes a [syncretic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syncretism "Syncretism") stage in the development of religions in late antiquity. A henotheist may worship a single god from a [pantheon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pantheon_\(gods\) "Pantheon (gods)") of deities at a given time, depending on his or her choice, while accepting other deities and concepts of god.[^taliaferro78-2] [^alonp370-6] Henotheism and inclusive monotheism are terms that refer to a middle position between unlimited polytheism and exclusive monotheism.[^britannicamonolatry-3]

Zoroastrianism is often regarded as one of the oldest monotheistic religions in the world. Although [Ahura Mazda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ahura_Mazda "Ahura Mazda") is the supreme god, Zoroastrianism believes in lesser divinities known as Yazatas.[^12] These [yazatas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yazatas "Yazatas") ("good agents") include [Anahita](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anahita "Anahita"), [Sraosha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sraosha "Sraosha"), [Mithra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mithra "Mithra"), [Rashnu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rashnu "Rashnu"), and [Tishtrya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tishtrya "Tishtrya"). According to some scholars, there are two issues that have long made it problematic to identify Zoroastrianism as true monotheism: the presence of lesser deities and dualism. But before hastening to conclude that the Amesha Spentas and the other yazatas compromise the purity of monotheism, we should consider that the other historical monotheisms too made room for other figures endowed with supernatural powers to bridge the gulf between the exalted, remote Creator God and the human world: the angels in all of them (whose conception in post-exilic Judaism was apparently developed after the pattern of the Amesha Spentas; Boyce and Grenet, 1991, 404–405), the saints and the Virgin Mary in several Christian churches, and the other persons of the Trinity in all of Christianity. Despite the vast differences with Zoroastrian theology, the common thread is that all these beings are subordinate to the Godhead as helpers or (in the case of the persons of the Trinity) co-equals, hence they do not pursue different interests and are worshiped jointly with the Godhead, not separately; therefore the supplicant's dilemma does not arise.[^13] Others such as [Richard Foltz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Foltz "Richard Foltz") has put forth evidence that [Iranians of Pre-Islamic era](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Iran "History of Iran") worshipped all these figures, especially Mithra and Anahita.[^14]

[Prods Oktor Skjærvø](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prods_Oktor_Skj%C3%A6rv%C3%B8 "Prods Oktor Skjærvø") states Zoroastrianism is henotheistic, and "a dualistic and polytheistic religion, but with one supreme god, who is the father of the ordered cosmos".[^15] Other scholars state that this is unclear, because historic texts present a conflicting picture, ranging from Zoroastrianism's belief in "one god, two gods, or a best god henotheism".[^16]

Henotheism was the term used by scholars such as [Max Müller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Max_M%C3%BCller "Max Müller") to describe the theology of [Vedic religion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Historical_Vedic_religion "Historical Vedic religion").[^max-19][^taliaferro78-2] Müller noted that the hymns of the *[Rigveda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rigveda "Rigveda")*, the oldest scripture of Hinduism, mention many deities, but praises them successively as the "one ultimate, supreme God", alternatively as "one supreme Goddess",[^20] thereby asserting that the essence of the deities was unitary (*[ekam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ekam "Ekam")*), and the deities were nothing but pluralistic manifestations of the same concept of the divine (God).[^taliaferro78-2][^alonp370-6][^fahlbusch524-9]

The Vedic era conceptualization of the divine or the One, states Jeaneane Fowler, is more abstract than a monotheistic God, it is the Reality behind and of the phenomenal universe.[^jeaneanefowler43-21] The Vedic hymns treat it as "limitless, indescribable, absolute principle", thus the Vedic divine is something of a [panentheism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Panentheism "Panentheism") rather than simple henotheism.[^jeaneanefowler43-21] In late Vedic era, around the start of Upanishadic age (~800 BCE), theosophical speculations emerge that develop concepts which scholars variously call [nondualism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nonduality_\(spirituality\) "Nonduality (spirituality)") or [monism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monism "Monism"), as well as forms of non-theism and [pantheism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pantheism "Pantheism").[^jeaneanefowler43-21][^22][^23] An example of the questioning of the concept of God, in addition to henotheistic hymns found therein, are in later portions of the *Rigveda*, such as the [Nasadiya Sukta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nasadiya_Sukta "Nasadiya Sukta").[^24] [Hinduism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hinduism "Hinduism") calls the metaphysical absolute concept as [Brahman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brahman "Brahman"), incorporating within it the [transcendent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transcendence_\(religion\) "Transcendence (religion)") and [immanent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Immanence "Immanence") reality.[^25][^26][^27] Different schools of thought interpret Brahman as either [personal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Personal_god "Personal god"), impersonal or transpersonal. Ishwar Chandra Sharma describes it as "Absolute Reality, beyond all dualities of existence and non-existence, light and darkness, and of time, space and cause."[^28]

## Hellenistic religion

While [Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_mythology "Greek mythology") and [Roman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_mythology "Roman mythology") religion began as [polytheism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polytheism "Polytheism"), during the [Classical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_antiquity "Classical antiquity") period, under the influence of philosophy, differing conceptions emerged. Often [Zeus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zeus "Zeus") (or [Jupiter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jupiter_\(god\) "Jupiter (god)")) was considered the supreme, all-powerful and all-knowing king and father of the Olympian gods. According to [Maijastina Kahlos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maijastina_Kahlos "Maijastina Kahlos"), "monotheism was pervasive in the educated circles in Late Antiquity" and "all divinities were interpreted as aspects, particles or epithets of one supreme God".[^maij-29] [Maximus Tyrius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maximus_Tyrius "Maximus Tyrius") (2nd century CE) stated: "In such a mighty contest, sedition and discord, you will see one according law and assertion in all the earth, that there is one God, the king and father of all things, and many gods, sons of God, ruling together with him."[^30] The [Neoplatonic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neoplatonic "Neoplatonic") philosopher [Plotinus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plotinus "Plotinus") taught that above the gods of traditional belief was "The One".[^maij-29] Maximus, the polytheist grammarian of [Madauros](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Madauros "Madauros"),[^maij-29] even stated that "only a madman would deny the existence of the supreme God".[^maij-29]

## Canaanite religion and Yahwism

[Second Temple Judaism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Temple_Judaism "Second Temple Judaism") and [Rabbinical Judaism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rabbinical_Judaism "Rabbinical Judaism") are emphatically monotheistic; however, its predecessor—the cult of [Yahweh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yahweh "Yahweh") as it was practiced in [ancient Israel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Israel_and_Judah "Ancient Israel and Judah") during the 9th and 8th centuries BCE ([Yahwism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yahwism "Yahwism"))—has been described as henotheistic or [monolatric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monolatric "Monolatric"). For example, the [Moabites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moab "Moab") worshipped the god [Chemosh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemosh "Chemosh"), the [Edomites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edom "Edom"), [Qaus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qaus "Qaus"), both of whom were part of the greater [Canaanite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canaan "Canaan") pantheon, headed by the chief god, [El](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/El_\(Canaanite_god\) "El (Canaanite god)"). The Canaanite pantheon consisted of [El](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/El_\(deity\) "El (deity)") and [Asherah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asherah "Asherah") as the chief deities, with 70 sons who were said to rule over each of the nations of the earth. These sons were each worshiped within a specific region. [Kurt Noll](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kurt_Noll "Kurt Noll") states that "the Bible preserves a tradition that Yahweh used to 'live' in the south, in the land of Edom" and that the original god of Israel was [El Shaddai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/El_Shaddai "El Shaddai").[^noll-31]

Several biblical stories allude to the belief that the Canaanite gods all existed and were thought to possess the most power in the lands by the people who worshiped them and their sacred objects; their power was believed to be real and could be invoked by the people who patronized them. There are numerous accounts of surrounding nations of Israel showing fear or reverence for the Israelite God despite their continued polytheistic practices.[^32] For instance, in 1 Samuel 4, the [Philistines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philistines "Philistines") fret before the second battle of [Aphek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aphek_\(biblical\) "Aphek (biblical)") when they learn that the Israelites are bearing the [Ark of the Covenant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ark_of_the_Covenant "Ark of the Covenant"), and therefore Yahweh, into battle. The Israelites were forbidden to worship other deities; according to some interpretations of the Bible, they were not fully monotheistic before the [Babylonian captivity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Babylonian_captivity "Babylonian captivity"). The biblical scholar [Mark S. Smith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mark_S._Smith "Mark S. Smith") refers to this stage as a form of [monolatry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monolatry "Monolatry").[^smith-33] Smith argues that Yahweh underwent a process of merging with El and that acceptance of cults of [Asherah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asherah "Asherah") was common in the period of the Judges.[^smith-33] 2 Kings 3:27 has been interpreted as describing a human sacrifice in Moab that led the invading Israelite army to fear the power of [Chemosh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemosh "Chemosh").[^boyd-34]

[Paul the Apostle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_the_Apostle "Paul the Apostle"), in his [First Epistle to the Corinthians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Epistle_to_the_Corinthians "First Epistle to the Corinthians"), writes that "we know that an idol is nothing" and "that there is none other God but one".[^35] He argues in verse 5 that "for though there be that are called gods, whether in heaven or in earth", "but to us there is but one God". Some translators of verse 5 put the words "gods" and "lords" in quotes to indicate that they are gods or lords only *so-called*.[^36]

In his [Second Epistle to the Corinthians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Epistle_to_the_Corinthians "Second Epistle to the Corinthians"), Paul refers to "the god of this world",[^37] which the 18th-century theologian [John Gill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Gill_\(theologian\) "John Gill (theologian)") interpreted as a reference to [Satan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satan "Satan") or the material things put before God, such as money, rather than acknowledging any separate deity from God.[^38]

### The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints

Some scholars have written that [the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Church_of_Jesus_Christ_of_Latter-day_Saints "The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints") (LDS Church) can be characterized as henotheistic but others have rejected that stance. [Eugene England](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eugene_England "Eugene England"), a professor at [Brigham Young University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brigham_Young_University "Brigham Young University"), asserted that LDS Presidents [Brigham Young](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brigham_Young "Brigham Young") and [Joseph Fielding Smith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Fielding_Smith "Joseph Fielding Smith") along with the LDS scholar [B. H. Roberts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/B._H._Roberts "B. H. Roberts") used the LDS interpretation of 1 Corinthians 8:5–6 as "a brief explanation of how it is possible to be both a Christian polytheist (technically a henotheist) and a monotheist".[^39] [BYU](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BYU "BYU") Professor Roger R. Keller rejected descriptions of the LDS Church as polytheistic by countering, as summarized by a reviewer, "Mormons are fundamentally monotheistic because they deal with only one god out of the many which exist."[^40]

In their book, *Mormon America: The Power and the Promise*, [Richard and Joan Ostling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_and_Joan_Ostling "Richard and Joan Ostling"), wrote that some Mormons are comfortable describing themselves as henotheists.[^41] Kurt Widmer, professor at the [University of Lethbridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Lethbridge "University of Lethbridge"), described LDS beliefs as a "cosmic henotheism".[^42] A review of Widmer's book by Bruening and Paulsen in the *FARMS Review of Books* countered that Widmer's hypothesis was "strongly disconfirmed in light of the total evidence".[^43] Van Hale wrote that "Mormonism teaches the existence of gods who are not the Father, Son, or Holy Ghost" and "the existence of more than one god \[is\] clearly a Mormon doctrine", but he also said that defining this belief system in theological terms was troublesome. According to Van Hale, henotheism might appear to be "promising" in describing LDS beliefs but is ultimately not accurate because henotheism was intended to describe the worship of a god that was restricted to a specific geographical area.[^44]
