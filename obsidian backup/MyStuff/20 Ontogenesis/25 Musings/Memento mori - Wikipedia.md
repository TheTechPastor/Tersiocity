***Memento mori*** (Latin for "remember that you shall die")[\[2\]](#cite_note-oed-2) is an artistic or symbolic trope acting as a reminder of the inevitability of death.[\[2\]](#cite_note-oed-2) The concept has its roots in the philosophers of [classical antiquity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_antiquity "Classical antiquity") and [Christianity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christianity "Christianity"), and appeared in [funerary art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Funerary_art "Funerary art") and architecture from the medieval period onwards.

The most common [motif](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Motif_(visual_arts) "Motif (visual arts)") is a skull, often accompanied by bones. Often this alone is enough to evoke the trope, but other motifs include a coffin, [hourglass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hourglass "Hourglass"), or wilting flowers to signify the impermanence of life. Often these function within a work whose main subject is something else, such as a portrait, but the *[vanitas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vanitas "Vanitas")* is an artistic genre where the theme of death is the main subject. The *[Danse Macabre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Danse_Macabre "Danse Macabre")* and death personified with a scythe as the [Grim Reaper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grim_Reaper "Grim Reaper") are even more direct evocations of the trope.

## Pronunciation and translation\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Memento_mori&action=edit&section=1 "Edit section: Pronunciation and translation")\]

In English, the phrase is typically pronounced , [*mə-MEN\-toh MOR\-ee*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:Pronunciation_respelling_key "Help:Pronunciation respelling key").

*Memento* is the [2nd person singular](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/second-person_singular "wikt:second-person singular") [active](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Active_voice "Active voice") [future](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Future_tense "Future tense") [imperative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imperative_mood "Imperative mood") of *meminī*, 'to remember, to bear in mind', usually serving as a warning: "remember!" *Morī* is the [present](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Present_tense "Present tense") [infinitive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infinitive "Infinitive") of the [deponent verb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deponent_verb "Deponent verb") *morior* 'to die'.[\[3\]](#cite_note-3) Thus, the phrase literally translates as "you must remember to die" but may be loosely rendered as "remember death" or "remember that you die".[\[4\]](#cite_note-4)

## History of the concept\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Memento_mori&action=edit&section=2 "Edit section: History of the concept")\]

### In classical antiquity\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Memento_mori&action=edit&section=3 "Edit section: In classical antiquity")\]

The philosopher [Democritus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democritus "Democritus") trained himself by going into solitude and frequenting tombs.[\[5\]](#cite_note-5) [Plato](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plato "Plato")'s *[Phaedo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phaedo "Phaedo")*, where the death of [Socrates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socrates "Socrates") is recounted, introduces the idea that the proper practice of philosophy is "about nothing else but dying and being dead".[\[6\]](#cite_note-6)

The [Stoics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stoics "Stoics") of [classical antiquity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_antiquity "Classical antiquity") were particularly prominent in their use of this discipline, and [Seneca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seneca_the_Younger "Seneca the Younger")'s letters are full of injunctions to meditate on death.[\[7\]](#cite_note-7) The Stoic [Epictetus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epictetus "Epictetus") told his students that when kissing their child, brother, or friend, they should remind themselves that they are mortal, curbing their pleasure, as do "those who stand behind men in their triumphs and remind them that they are mortal".[\[8\]](#cite_note-8) The Stoic [Marcus Aurelius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marcus_Aurelius "Marcus Aurelius") invited the reader (himself) to "consider how ephemeral and mean all mortal things are" in his *[Meditations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meditations "Meditations")*.[\[9\]](#cite_note-9)[\[10\]](#cite_note-10)

In some accounts of the [Roman triumph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_triumph "Roman triumph"), a companion or [public slave](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Servus_publicus "Servus publicus") would stand behind or near the [triumphant general](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Triumphator "Triumphator") during the procession and remind him from time to time of his own mortality or prompt him to "look behind".[\[11\]](#cite_note-11) A version of this warning is often rendered into English as "Remember, Caesar, thou art mortal", for example in *[Fahrenheit 451](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fahrenheit_451 "Fahrenheit 451")*.

### In early Christianity\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Memento_mori&action=edit&section=4 "Edit section: In early Christianity")\]

Several passages in the Old Testament urge a remembrance of death. In [Psalm 90](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psalm_90 "Psalm 90"), Moses prays that God would teach his people "to number our days that we may get a heart of wisdom" ([Ps. 90:12](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Bible_(King_James)/Psalms#90:12 "s:Bible (King James)/Psalms")). In [Ecclesiastes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecclesiastes_7 "Ecclesiastes 7"), the Preacher insists that "It is better to go to the house of mourning than to go to the house of feasting, for this is the end of all mankind, and the living will lay it to heart" ([Eccl. 7:2](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Bible_(King_James)/Ecclesiastes#7:2 "s:Bible (King James)/Ecclesiastes")). In Isaiah, the lifespan of human beings is compared to the short lifespan of grass: "The grass withers, the flower fades when the breath of the LORD blows on it; surely [the people are grass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/All_flesh_is_grass "All flesh is grass")" ([Is. 40:7](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isaiah_40:7 "Isaiah 40:7")).

The expression *memento mori* developed with the growth of Christianity, which emphasized [Heaven](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heaven "Heaven"), [Hell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hell "Hell"), [Hades](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_views_on_Hades "Christian views on Hades") and salvation of the soul in the [afterlife](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Afterlife "Afterlife").[\[12\]](#cite_note-12) The 2nd-century Christian writer [Tertullian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tertullian "Tertullian") claimed in his [Apologeticus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apologeticus "Apologeticus"), that during a [triumphal procession](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_triumph "Roman triumph"), a victorious general had someone standing behind him, holding a crown over his head and whispering: "*Respice post te. Hominem te esse memento. Memento mori.*" ("Look after yourself. Remember you're a man. Remember you will die."). Though in modern times this has become a standard [trope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trope_(literature) "Trope (literature)"), in fact no other ancient authors confirm this, and it may have been Christian moralizing on Tertullians part rather than an accurate historical report.[\[13\]](#cite_note-13)

### In Europe from the medieval era to the Victorian era\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Memento_mori&action=edit&section=5 "Edit section: In Europe from the medieval era to the Victorian era")\]

[![[9799c33ef409062632dabf34921881ba_MD5.jpg]]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Dance_of_Death_(replica_of_15th_century_fresco;_National_Gallery_of_Slovenia).jpg "Dance of Death (replica of 15th-century fresco; National Gallery of Slovenia); No matter one's station in life, the Dance of Death unites all.")

*[Dance of Death](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Danse_Macabre "Danse Macabre")* (replica of 15th-century fresco; National Gallery of Slovenia); No matter one's station in life, the Dance of Death unites all.

#### Christian Theology\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Memento_mori&action=edit&section=6 "Edit section: Christian Theology")\]

The thought was then utilized in Christianity, whose strong emphasis on [divine judgment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Divine_judgment "Divine judgment"), [heaven](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heaven_(Christianity) "Heaven (Christianity)"), [hell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hell_in_Christian_beliefs "Hell in Christian beliefs"), and the [salvation of the soul](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salvation_in_Christianity "Salvation in Christianity") brought death to the forefront of consciousness.[\[14\]](#cite_note-14) In the Christian context, the *memento mori* acquires a moralizing purpose quite opposed to the *[nunc est bibendum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nunc_est_bibendum "Nunc est bibendum")* (now is the time to drink) theme of [classical antiquity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_antiquity "Classical antiquity"). To the Christian, the prospect of death serves to emphasize the emptiness and fleetingness of earthly pleasures, luxuries, and achievements, and thus also as an invitation to focus one's thoughts on the prospect of the afterlife. A Biblical injunction often associated with the *memento mori* in this context is *In omnibus operibus tuis memorare novissima tua, et in aeternum non peccabis* (the [Vulgate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vulgate "Vulgate")'s Latin rendering of [Ecclesiasticus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sirach "Sirach") [7:40](https://bible.oremus.org/?passage=Ecclesiasticus%207:40&version=nrsvae), "in all thy works be mindful of thy last end and thou wilt never sin.") This finds ritual expression in the rites of [Ash Wednesday](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ash_Wednesday "Ash Wednesday"), when ashes are placed upon the worshipers' heads with the words, "Remember Man that you are dust and unto dust, you shall return."

*Memento mori* has been an important part of [ascetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ascetic "Ascetic") disciplines as a means of perfecting the character by cultivating detachment and other virtues, and by turning the attention towards the [immortality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Immortality "Immortality") of the soul and the afterlife.[\[15\]](#cite_note-15)

#### Architecture\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Memento_mori&action=edit&section=7 "Edit section: Architecture")\]

[![[587757da72b400435745f3ef6112acc3_MD5.jpg]]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:TombStJohnsChester.JPG)

Unshrouded skeleton on [Diana Warburton](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Diana_Warburton&action=edit&redlink=1 "Diana Warburton (page does not exist)")'s tomb (dated 1693) in [St John the Baptist Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_John_the_Baptist%27s_Church,_Chester "St John the Baptist's Church, Chester"), [Chester](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chester "Chester")

The most obvious places to look for *memento mori* meditations are in funeral art and [architecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Architecture "Architecture"). Perhaps the most striking to contemporary minds is the *transi* or *[cadaver tomb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cadaver_tomb "Cadaver tomb")*, a tomb that depicts the decayed [corpse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dead_body "Dead body") of the deceased. This became a fashion in the tombs of the wealthy in the fifteenth century, and surviving examples still offer a stark reminder of the vanity of earthly riches. Later, [Puritan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Puritan "Puritan") [tomb stones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tomb_stone "Tomb stone") in the colonial United States frequently depicted winged skulls, skeletons, or [angels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angel "Angel") snuffing out candles. These are among the numerous [themes associated with skull imagery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skull_(mythology) "Skull (mythology)").

Another example of *memento mori* is provided by the chapels of bones, such as the [Capela dos Ossos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capela_dos_Ossos "Capela dos Ossos") in [Évora](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%89vora "Évora") or the [Capuchin Crypt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capuchin_Crypt "Capuchin Crypt") in Rome. These are chapels where the walls are totally or partially covered by human remains, mostly bones. The entrance to the Capela dos Ossos has the following sentence: "We bones, lying here bare, await yours."

#### Visual art\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Memento_mori&action=edit&section=8 "Edit section: Visual art")\]

[![[70a93af587cde91d0a3b070fdf50d39d_MD5.jpg]]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:StillLifeWithASkull.jpg)

[Philippe de Champaigne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philippe_de_Champaigne "Philippe de Champaigne")'s *[Vanitas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vanitas "Vanitas")* (c. 1671) is reduced to three essentials: Life, Death, and Time

Timepieces have been used to illustrate that the time of the living on Earth grows shorter with each passing minute. Public [clocks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clock "Clock") would be decorated with mottos such as *ultima forsan* ("perhaps the last" \[hour\]) or *vulnerant omnes, ultima necat* ("they all wound, and the last kills"). Clocks have carried the motto *[tempus fugit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tempus_fugit "Tempus fugit")*, "time flees". Old striking clocks often sported [automata](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Automaton "Automaton") who would appear and strike the hour; some of the celebrated automaton clocks from [Augsburg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Augsburg "Augsburg"), Germany, had Death striking the hour. Private people carried smaller reminders of their own mortality. [Mary, Queen of Scots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mary,_Queen_of_Scots "Mary, Queen of Scots") owned a large watch carved in the form of a [silver](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silver "Silver") skull, embellished with the lines of [Horace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horace "Horace"), "Pale death knocks with the same tempo upon the huts of the poor and the towers of Kings."

In the late 16th and through the 17th century, *memento mori* jewelry was popular. Items included [mourning rings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mourning_ring "Mourning ring"),[\[16\]](#cite_note-Taylor-16) [pendants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pendants "Pendants"), [lockets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Locket "Locket"), and [brooches](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brooch "Brooch").[\[17\]](#cite_note-AJU-17) These pieces depicted tiny motifs of skulls, bones, and coffins, in addition to messages and names of the departed, picked out in [precious metals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Precious_metals "Precious metals") and [enamel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vitreous_enamel "Vitreous enamel").[\[17\]](#cite_note-AJU-17)[\[18\]](#cite_note-18)

During the same period there emerged the artistic genre known as *[vanitas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vanitas "Vanitas")*, Latin for "emptiness" or "vanity". Especially popular in [Holland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holland "Holland") and then spreading to other [European nations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_nations "European nations"), vanitas paintings typically represented assemblages of numerous symbolic objects such as human skulls, guttering candles, wilting flowers, soap bubbles, butterflies, and hourglasses. In combination, vanitas assemblies conveyed the impermanence of human endeavours and of the decay that is inevitable with the passage of time. See also the themes associated with [the image of the skull](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skull_(symbolism) "Skull (symbolism)"). The 2007 [screenprint](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Screen_printing "Screen printing") by the street-artist [Banksy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Banksy "Banksy") "Grin Reaper" features the [Grim Reaper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Death_(personification) "Death (personification)") with acid-house smiley face sitting on a clock demonstrating death awaiting us all.[\[19\]](#cite_note-19)

#### Literature\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Memento_mori&action=edit&section=9 "Edit section: Literature")\]

*Memento mori* is also an important literary theme. Well-known literary meditations on death in English prose include Sir [Thomas Browne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Browne "Thomas Browne")'s *[Hydriotaphia, Urn Burial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydriotaphia,_Urn_Burial "Hydriotaphia, Urn Burial")* and [Jeremy Taylor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jeremy_Taylor "Jeremy Taylor")'s *[Holy Living and Holy Dying](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holy_Living_and_Holy_Dying "Holy Living and Holy Dying")*. These works were part of a [Jacobean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacobean_era "Jacobean era") [cult of melancholia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Melancholia "Melancholia") that marked the end of the [Elizabethan era](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elizabethan_era "Elizabethan era"). In the late eighteenth century, literary [elegies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elegy "Elegy") were a common genre; [Thomas Gray](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Gray "Thomas Gray")'s *[Elegy Written in a Country Churchyard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elegy_Written_in_a_Country_Churchyard "Elegy Written in a Country Churchyard")* and [Edward Young](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Young "Edward Young")'s *Night Thoughts* are typical members of the genre.

In the European devotional literature of the Renaissance, the *[Ars Moriendi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ars_Moriendi "Ars Moriendi")*, *memento mori* had moral value by reminding individuals of their mortality.[\[20\]](#cite_note-20)

#### Music\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Memento_mori&action=edit&section=10 "Edit section: Music")\]

Apart from the genre of [requiem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Music_for_the_Requiem_Mass "Music for the Requiem Mass") and funeral music, there is also a rich tradition of *memento mori* in the [Early Music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_Music "Early Music") of Europe. Especially those facing the ever-present death during the recurring [bubonic plague](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bubonic_plague "Bubonic plague") [pandemics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pandemic "Pandemic") from the 1340s onward tried to toughen themselves by anticipating the inevitable in [chants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chants "Chants"), from the simple [Geisslerlieder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geisslerlieder "Geisslerlieder") of the [Flagellant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flagellant "Flagellant") movement to the more refined cloistral or courtly songs. The lyrics often looked at life as a necessary and god-given [vale of tears](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vale_of_tears "Vale of tears") with death as a ransom, and they reminded people to lead sinless lives to stand a chance at [Judgment Day](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Last_Judgment "Last Judgment"). The following two Latin stanzas (with their English translations) are typical of *memento mori* in medieval music; they are from the [virelai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virelai "Virelai") *[Ad Mortem Festinamus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ad_Mortem_Festinamus "Ad Mortem Festinamus")* of the [Llibre Vermell de Montserrat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Llibre_Vermell_de_Montserrat "Llibre Vermell de Montserrat") from 1399:

Vita brevis breviter in brevi finietur,  
Mors venit velociter quae neminem veretur,  
Omnia mors perimit et nulli miseretur.  
Ad mortem festinamus peccare desistamus.

Ni conversus fueris et sicut puer factus  
Et vitam mutaveris in meliores actus,  
Intrare non poteris regnum Dei beatus.  
Ad mortem festinamus peccare desistamus.

Life is short, and shortly it will end;  
Death comes quickly and respects no one,  
Death destroys everything and takes pity on no one.  
To death we are hastening, let us refrain from sinning.

If you do not turn back and become like a child,  
And change your life for the better,  
You will not be able to enter, blessed, the Kingdom of God.  
To death we are hastening, let us refrain from sinning.

#### Danse macabre\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Memento_mori&action=edit&section=11 "Edit section: Danse macabre")\]

The *[danse macabre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Danse_macabre "Danse macabre")* is another well-known example of the *memento mori* theme, with its dancing depiction of the [Grim Reaper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Death_(personification) "Death (personification)") carrying off rich and poor alike. This and similar depictions of Death decorated many European churches.

### The salutation of the Hermits of St. Paul of France\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Memento_mori&action=edit&section=13 "Edit section: The salutation of the Hermits of St. Paul of France")\]

*Memento mori* was the salutation used by the [Hermits of St. Paul of France](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermits_of_St._Paul_of_France "Hermits of St. Paul of France") (1620–1633), also known as the Brothers of Death.[\[21\]](#cite_note-21) It is sometimes claimed that the [Trappists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trappists "Trappists") use this salutation, but this is not true.[\[22\]](#cite_note-22)

### In Puritan America\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Memento_mori&action=edit&section=14 "Edit section: In Puritan America")\]

[![[2b857751e40ae6c5cff65003498e42a8_MD5.jpg]]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Thomas_Smith_001.jpg)

[Thomas Smith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Smith_(American_painter) "Thomas Smith (American painter)")'s *Self-Portrait*

[Colonial American art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colonial_American_art "Colonial American art") saw a large number of *memento mori* images due to [Puritan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Puritan "Puritan") influence. The Puritan community in 17th-century North America looked down upon art because they believed that it drew the faithful away from God and, if away from God, then it could only lead to the devil. However, portraits were considered historical records and, as such, they were allowed. [Thomas Smith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Smith_(American_painter) "Thomas Smith (American painter)"), a 17th-century Puritan, fought in many naval battles and also painted. In his self-portrait, we see these pursuits represented alongside a typical Puritan *memento mori* with a skull, suggesting his awareness of imminent death.

The poem underneath the skull emphasizes Thomas Smith's acceptance of death and of turning away from the world of the living:

> Why why should I the World be minding, Therein a World of Evils Finding. Then Farwell World: Farwell thy jarres, thy Joies thy Toies thy Wiles thy Warrs. Truth Sounds Retreat: I am not sorye. The Eternall Drawes to him my heart, By Faith (which can thy Force Subvert) To Crowne me (after Grace) with Glory.

### Mexico's Day of the Dead\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Memento_mori&action=edit&section=15 "Edit section: Mexico's Day of the Dead")\]

[![[2bd598f1d75614cdfa22046758895b1b_MD5.jpg]]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Posada2.Catrina.jpeg)

[Posada's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jos%C3%A9_Guadalupe_Posada "José Guadalupe Posada") 1910 *[La Calavera Catrina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/La_Calavera_Catrina "La Calavera Catrina")*

Much *memento mori* art is associated with the [Mexican festival](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mexican_culture "Mexican culture") [Day of the Dead](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Day_of_the_Dead "Day of the Dead"), including skull-shaped candies and bread loaves adorned with bread "bones".

This theme was also famously expressed in the works of the Mexican engraver [José Guadalupe Posada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jos%C3%A9_Guadalupe_Posada "José Guadalupe Posada"), in which people from various walks of life are depicted as skeletons.

Another manifestation of *memento mori* is found in the Mexican "Calavera", a literary composition in verse form normally written in honour of a person who is still alive, but written as if that person were dead. These compositions have a comedic tone and are often offered from one friend to another during [Day of the Dead](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Day_of_the_Dead "Day of the Dead").[\[23\]](#cite_note-23)

### Contemporary culture\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Memento_mori&action=edit&section=16 "Edit section: Contemporary culture")\]

[Roman Krznaric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Krznaric "Roman Krznaric") suggests Memento Mori is an important topic to bring back into our thoughts and belief system; "Philosophers have come up with lots of what I call 'death tasters' – thought experiments for seizing the day."

These thought experiments are powerful to get us re-oriented back to death into current awareness and living with spontaneity. [Albert Camus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albert_Camus "Albert Camus") stated "Come to terms with death, thereafter anything is possible." [Jean-Paul Sartre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean-Paul_Sartre "Jean-Paul Sartre") expressed that life is given to us early, and is shortened at the end, all the while taken away at every step of the way, emphasizing that the end is only the beginning every day.[\[24\]](#cite_note-Seize_the_day-24)

## Similar concepts across cultures\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Memento_mori&action=edit&section=17 "Edit section: Similar concepts across cultures")\]

### In Buddhism\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Memento_mori&action=edit&section=18 "Edit section: In Buddhism")\]

The Buddhist practice [*maraṇasati*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maranasati "Maranasati") meditates on death. The word is a [Pāli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P%C4%81li "Pāli") compound of *maraṇa* 'death' (an [Indo-European](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indo-European "Indo-European") cognate of Latin *mori*) and *sati* 'awareness', so very close to *memento mori*. It is first used in early Buddhist texts, the [suttapiṭaka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sutta_Pitaka "Sutta Pitaka") of the [Pāli Canon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P%C4%81li_Canon "Pāli Canon"), with parallels in the [āgamas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C4%80gama_(Buddhism) "Āgama (Buddhism)") of the "Northern" Schools.

### In Japanese Zen and samurai culture\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Memento_mori&action=edit&section=19 "Edit section: In Japanese Zen and samurai culture")\]

In Japan, the influence of [Zen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zen "Zen") Buddhist contemplation of death on indigenous culture can be gauged by the following quotation from the classic treatise on [samurai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samurai "Samurai") ethics, *[Hagakure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hagakure "Hagakure")*:[\[25\]](#cite_note-25)

> The Way of the Samurai is, morning after morning, the practice of death, considering whether it will be here or be there, imagining the most sightly way of dying, and putting one's mind firmly in death. Although this may be a most difficult thing, if one will do it, it can be done. There is nothing that one should suppose cannot be done.[\[26\]](#cite_note-26)

In the annual appreciation of [cherry blossom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cherry_blossom "Cherry blossom") and fall colors, *[hanami](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hanami "Hanami")* and *[momijigari](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leaf_peeping "Leaf peeping")*, it was philosophized that things are most splendid at the moment before their fall, and to aim to live and die in a similar fashion.\[*[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*\]

### In Tibetan Buddhism\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Memento_mori&action=edit&section=20 "Edit section: In Tibetan Buddhism")\]

[![[2e056c81374cb42776a7c3f0d2dde86f_MD5.jpg]]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Tibetan_Mask.jpg)

Tibetan [Citipati](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Citipati_(Buddhism) "Citipati (Buddhism)") mask depicting [Mahākāla](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mah%C4%81k%C4%81la "Mahākāla"). The skull mask of Citipati is a reminder of the impermanence of life and the eternal cycle of life and death.

In Tibetan Buddhism, there is a mind training practice known as [Lojong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lojong "Lojong"). The initial stages of the classic Lojong begin with 'The Four Thoughts that Turn the Mind', or, more literally, 'Four Contemplations to Cause a Revolution in the Mind'.\[*[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*\] The second of these four is the contemplation on impermanence and death. In particular, one contemplates that;

-   All compounded things are impermanent.
-   The human body is a compounded thing.
-   Therefore, death of the body is certain.
-   The time of death is uncertain and beyond our control.

There are a number of classic verse formulations of these contemplations meant for daily reflection to overcome our strong habitual tendency to live as though we will certainly not die today.

#### Lalitavistara Sutra\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Memento_mori&action=edit&section=21 "Edit section: Lalitavistara Sutra")\]

The following is from the [Lalitavistara Sūtra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lalitavistara_S%C5%ABtra "Lalitavistara Sūtra"), a major work in the classical Sanskrit canon:

ज्वलितं त्रिभवं जरव्याधिदुखैः मरणाग्निप्रदीप्तमनाथमिदम्।  
भवनि शरणे सद मूढ जगत् भ्रमती भ्रमरो यथ कुम्भगतो॥

अध्रुवं त्रिभवं शरदभ्रनिभं नटरङ्गसमा जगिर् ऊर्मिच्युती।  
गिरिनद्यसमं लघुशीघ्रजवं व्रजतायु जगे यथ विद्यु नभे॥

Beings are ablaze with the sufferings of sickness and old age,  
And with no defence against the conflagration of Death  
The bewildered, seeking refuge in worldly existence  
Spin round and round, like bees trapped in a jar.

The three worlds are fleeting like autumn clouds.  
Like a staged performance, beings come and go.  
In tumultuous waves, rushing by, like rapids over a cliff.  
Like lightning, wanderers in samsara burst into existence, and are gone in a flash.[\[27\]](#cite_note-27)

#### The Udānavarga\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Memento_mori&action=edit&section=22 "Edit section: The Udānavarga")\]

A very well known verse in the Pali, Sanskrit and Tibetan canons states \[this is from the Sanskrit version, the [Udānavarga](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Ud%C4%81navarga&action=edit&redlink=1 "Udānavarga (page does not exist)"):

सर्वे क्षयान्ता निचयाः पतनान्ताः समुच्छ्रयाः |  
सम्योगा विप्रयोगान्ता मरणान्तं हि जीवितम् |1,22|

All that is acquired will be lost  
What rises will fall  
Where there is meeting there will be separation  
What is born will surely die.[\[28\]](#cite_note-28)

#### Shantideva, Bodhicaryavatara\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Memento_mori&action=edit&section=23 "Edit section: Shantideva, Bodhicaryavatara")\]

[Shantideva](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shantideva "Shantideva"), in the [Bodhisattvacaryāvatāra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bodhisattvacary%C4%81vat%C4%81ra "Bodhisattvacaryāvatāra") 'Bodhisattva's Way of Life' reflects at length:

कृताकृतापरीक्षोऽयं मृत्युर्विश्रम्भघातकः।  
स्वस्थास्वस्थैरविश्वास्य आकमिस्मकमहाशनि:॥  
२/३४॥

अप्रिया न भविष्यन्ति प्रियो मे न भविष्यति।  
अहं च न भविष्यामि सर्वं च न भविष्यति॥  
२/३७॥

तत्तत्स्मरणताम याति यद्यद्वस्त्वनुभयते।  
स्वप्नानुभूतवत्सर्वं गतं न पूनरीक्ष्यते॥  
२/३६॥

रात्रिन्दिवमविश्राममायुषो वर्धते व्ययः।  
आयस्य चागमो नास्ति न मरिष्यामि किं न्वहम्॥  
२/४०

यमदूतैर्गृहीतस्य कुतो बन्धुः कुतः सुह्रत्। पुण्यमेकं तदा त्राणं मया तच्च न सेवितम्॥  
२/४१॥

Death does not differentiate between tasks done and undone.  
This traitor is not to be trusted by the healthy or the ill,  
for it is like an unexpected, great thunderbolt.  
BCA 2.33

My enemies will not remain, nor will my friends remain.  
I shall not remain. Nothing will remain.  
BCA 2:35

Whatever is experienced will fade to a memory.  
Like an experience in a dream,  
everything that has passed will not be seen again.  
BCA 2:36

Day and night, a life span unceasingly diminishes,  
and there is no adding onto it. Shall I not die then?  
BCA 2:39

For a person seized by the messengers of Death,  
what good is a relative and what good is a friend?  
At that time, merit alone is a protection,  
and I have not applied myself to it.  
BCA 2:41

#### In more modern Tibetan Buddhist works\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Memento_mori&action=edit&section=24 "Edit section: In more modern Tibetan Buddhist works")\]

In a practice text written by the 19th century Tibetan master [Dudjom Lingpa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dudjom_Lingpa "Dudjom Lingpa") for serious [meditators](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Meditators&action=edit&redlink=1 "Meditators (page does not exist)"), he formulates the second contemplation in this way:[\[29\]](#cite_note-29)[\[30\]](#cite_note-30)

> On this occasion when you have such a bounty of opportunities in terms of your body, environment, friends, spiritual mentors, time, and practical instructions, without procrastinating until tomorrow and the next day, arouse a sense of urgency, as if a spark landed on your body or a grain of sand fell in your eye. If you have not swiftly applied yourself to practice, examine the births and deaths of other beings and reflect again and again on the unpredictability of your lifespan and the time of your death, and on the uncertainty of your own situation. Meditate on this until you have definitively integrated it with your mind... The appearances of this life, including your surroundings and friends, are like last night's dream, and this life passes more swiftly than a flash of lightning in the sky. There is no end to this meaningless work. What a joke to prepare to live forever! Wherever you are born in the heights or depths of saṃsāra, the great noose of suffering will hold you tight. Acquiring freedom for yourself is as rare as a star in the daytime, so how is it possible to practice and achieve liberation? The root of all mind training and practical instructions is planted by knowing the nature of existence. There is no other way. I, an old vagabond, have shaken my beggar's satchel, and this is what came out.

The contemporary Tibetan master, [Yangthang Rinpoche](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yangthang_Rinpoche "Yangthang Rinpoche"), in his short text 'Summary of the View, Meditation, and Conduct':[\[31\]](#cite_note-31)

།ཁྱེད་རྙེད་དཀའ་བ་མི་ཡི་ལུས་རྟེན་རྙེད། །སྐྱེ་དཀའ་བའི་ངེས་འབྱུང་གི་བསམ་པ་སྐྱེས། །མཇལ་དཀའ་བའི་མཚན་ལྡན་གྱི་བླ་མ་མཇལ། །འཕྲད་དཀའ་བ་དམ་པའི་ཆོས་དང་འཕྲད།  
འདི་འདྲ་བའི་ལུས་རྟེན་བཟང་པོ་འདི། །ཐོབ་དཀའ་བའི་ཚུལ་ལ་ཡང་ཡང་སོམ། རྙེད་པ་འདི་དོན་ཡོད་མ་བྱས་ན། །འདི་མི་རྟག་རླུང་གསེབ་མར་མེ་འདྲ།  
ཡུན་རིང་པོའི་བློ་གཏད་འདི་ལ་མེད། །ཤི་བར་དོར་གྲོལ་བའི་གདེངས་མེད་ན། །ཚེ་ཕྱི་མའི་སྡུག་བསྔལ་ཨ་རེ་འཇིགས། །མཐའ་མེད་པའི་འཁོར་བར་འཁྱམས་དགོས་ཚེ།  
།འདིའི་རང་བཞིན་བསམ་ན་སེམས་རེ་སྐྱོ། །ཚེ་འདི་ལ་བློ་གདེངས་ཐོབ་པ་ཞིག །ཅི་ནས་ཀྱང་མཛད་རྒྱུ་བཀའ་དྲིན་ཆེ། །འདི་བདག་གིས་ཁྱོད་ལ་རེ་བ་ཡིན།

You have obtained a human life, which is difficult to find,  
Have aroused an intention of a spirit of emergence, which is difficult to arouse,  
Have met a qualified guru, who is difficult to meet,  
And you have encountered the sublime Dharma, which is difficult to encounter.  
Reflect again and again on the difficulty Of obtaining such a fine human life.  
If you do not make this meaningful,  
It will be like a butter lamp in the wind of impermanence.  
Do not count on this lasting a long time.

The Tibetan Canon also includes copious materials on the meditative preparation for the death process and intermediate period [bardo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bardo "Bardo") between death and rebirth. Amongst them are the famous "Tibetan Book of the Dead", in Tibetan *[Bardo Thodol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bardo_Thodol "Bardo Thodol")*, the "Natural Liberation through Hearing in the Bardo".

### In Islam\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Memento_mori&action=edit&section=25 "Edit section: In Islam")\]

The "remembrance of death" ([Arabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic_language "Arabic language"): تذكرة الموت, *Tadhkirat al-Mawt*; deriving from تذكرة, *tadhkirah*, Arabic for *memorandum* or *admonition*), has been a major topic of Islamic spirituality since the time of the Islamic prophet [Muhammad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muhammad "Muhammad") in [Medina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medina "Medina"). It is grounded in the [Qur'an](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qur%27an "Qur'an"), where there are recurring injunctions to pay heed to the fate of previous generations.[\[32\]](#cite_note-32) The [hadith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hadith "Hadith") literature, which preserves the teachings of Muhammad, records advice for believers to "remember often death, the destroyer of pleasures."[\[33\]](#cite_note-33) Some [Sufis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sufism "Sufism") have been called "ahl al-qubur," the "people of the graves," because of their practice of frequenting graveyards to ponder on mortality and the vanity of life, based on the teaching of Muhammad to visit graves.[\[34\]](#cite_note-34) [Al-Ghazali](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Ghazali "Al-Ghazali") devotes to this topic the last book of his "[The Revival of the Religious Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Revival_of_the_Religious_Sciences "The Revival of the Religious Sciences")".[\[35\]](#cite_note-35)

### Iceland\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Memento_mori&action=edit&section=26 "Edit section: Iceland")\]

The [Hávamál](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/H%C3%A1vam%C3%A1l "Hávamál") ("Sayings of the High One"), a 13th-century Icelandic compilation poetically attributed to the god [Odin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Odin "Odin"), includes two sections – the Gestaþáttr and the Loddfáfnismál – offering many [gnomic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gnomic_poetry "Gnomic poetry") proverbs expressing the memento mori philosophy, most famously Gestaþáttr number 77:

Deyr fé,  
deyja frændur,  
deyr sjálfur ið sama;  
ek veit einn at aldri deyr,  
dómr um dauðan hvern.

Animals die,  
friends die,  
and thyself, too, shall die;  
but one thing I know that never dies  
the tales of the one who died.
