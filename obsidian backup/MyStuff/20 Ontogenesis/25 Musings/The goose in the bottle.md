# The goose in the bottle

Here’s a riddle, adapted from a Zen koan: imagine you’ve come into possession of a live goose, trapped in a large glass bottle. (Don’t ask how or why.) The animal has plenty of room in the bottle, and air to breathe, but the neck of the bottle is much too narrow for the goose to pass through. Your job is to remove the goose from the bottle without harming it, and without breaking any glass.

Perhaps you’ll laugh this off as obviously impossible. Or narrow your eyes and furrow your brow, as you try to figure out what you’re missing. In fact, the problem is neither impossible nor difficult. It’s easy. First, imagine the goose is outside the bottle.

Actually, there are no further steps. That’s it. You did it!

I know, I know: incredibly annoying. But I think the goose-in-the-bottle scenario encapsulates a crucial and liberating lesson about the way that a certain kind of person – me, and maybe you too – tends to overcomplicate things, when it comes to meaningful productivity or psychological growth. 

As the therapists Bruce Ecker and Laurel Hulley point out in their 1996 book _Depth-Oriented Brief Therapy_, where I encountered the goose puzzle, the solution above isn’t a trick. You were asked to imagine a goose in a bottle. Then, without realizing it, you decided that imaginary geese in imaginary bottles must obey the physical laws that constrain real geese in the real world. And thus you thought yourself into a dead end. 

In truth, the puzzle “never consisted of a physical goose in a physical bottle and was not governed by the same properties as physical objects,” Ecker and Hulley write. “If the reader construed the imaginary goose and bottle as having the properties that a physical goose and bottle would have, the reader unconsciously invented a world and then took it to be real.”

One point they pursue throughout their (fascinating) book is that many of the barriers we experience, in our efforts to use our time fulfillingly and well, work in a similar way. They arise from unspoken frameworks of belief that we actively maintain in our lives, just as the person confronted with the goose-in-the-bottle problem actively imposes real-world physics on an imaginary goose. 

And so the liberating possibility here is that getting past those stifling barriers might be as easy as it was to free the imaginary goose, once you realised it didn’t need to obey the laws of physics. In other words: effortless. Once you see what’s going on, the problem just loses its reality.

They tell the story of a therapy patient who couldn’t make an inch of progress on his postgraduate work. The temptation for a hypothetical bad therapist here would be to treat the symptom – in this case, procrastination – as merely an irrational failing or error, and to try to find ways to help the patient overpower it. But Ecker and Hulley (whose approach is now known as [coherence therapy](https://coherencetherapy.org/)) argue that there’s always what they call a “pro-symptom position” – an unspoken stance, on the part of the patient, which causes them to keep on actively making the symptom occur. 

As they tell it, in the procrastinator’s case, it didn’t take much probing to reveal that academic success was really his parents’ ambition for him, not his own. It was “urgently important to him not to submit to a life program that he himself didn’t actually want.” His procrastination was serving a purpose. 

Another couple complained that they always seemed to ruin periods of marital harmony with blazing rows. It turned out that they feared losing their autonomy in the relationship. Fighting helped them feel like individuals again. In both cases, once these people saw what they were really up to, psychologically speaking, they were free to move forward. The procrastinator quit academia, and the couple found alternative, less fighty ways to feel a sense of independence. 

I realize that therapy success stories recounted like this can sound glib and unconvincing. But I think these ones make an important point: if you can figure out your motivation for continuing to do the things you wish you didn’t do – if you can become conscious of the “pro-symptom position” behind your procrastination, or imposter syndrome, or commitment-phobia, or addiction to distraction, or anything else – then change needn’t necessarily be difficult or slow. The psychological framework that’s constraining you derives its power from not being seen. Once you see it, the power reverts immediately to you. 

The broader point is that things you’d like to change can change faster than you think. There’s a sort of intellectual prejudice – of which I’m sure I’ve been guilty myself – that just because fist-pumping, affirmation-spouting motivational gurus offering quick fixes have so little to offer, real progress must therefore always be painful and slow. 

But self-help quick fixes are bogus because they’re _shallow._ Not because change can’t happen fast. If you’re willing and able to get deep and honest about why you do the counterproductive, happiness-limiting things you do, there’s no automatic reason why it might not be possible to walk away from them today.
