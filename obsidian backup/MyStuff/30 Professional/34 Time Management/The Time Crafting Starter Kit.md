### The idea behind Time Theming is a simple one.

Basically, when you theme your time you are assigning a specific timeframe an
overarching focus. These time frames are as follows:

I will go over each of these timeframes in a moment but I wanted to clarify
what giving a period of time a theme is and is not.

Giving a period of time a theme does not mean that you only focus on the
assigned theme during those periods of time. The idea is that should you get
distracted or diverted, you have a place for your attention to go to in a quick
and simple way.

Let me give you an example.

If I decide that my Daily Theme for Thursday is maintenance tasks (catching up
on email, updating my personal budget, filling out reports, etc.) I will
concentrate on trying to get those tasks done on that day as much as possible.
But if I get an email from a colleague that is asking me to do something that I
wouldn't necessarily classify as maintenance, I can take care of that and then
not get stuck with what to focus on afterward. Why? Because all I need to do
is look at my calendar to see that my overarching focus for Thursdays should
be maintenance tasks. I can get back to directing my attention towards those
types of tasks no matter what disruptions I face during the day.

If that sounds confusing, read it back to yourself again. If you think of theming
your time as a way to direct your attention by using your calendar as a
compass it will become clearer to you.

I think it's important not to try to incorporate all four time frames when you get
started. Start with the one that makes the most sense to you. In this guide I've
given you two worksheets that will help you decide how to theme your days
and your months. (However, if you modify the matrix that will help you choose
your Daily Themes you can also use it for Horizontal Themes and if you modify
the matrix that will help you decide your Monthly Themes it will help you
decide any Weekly Sprints that you may want to take on.)

I will briefly explain how Time Theming works for each timeframe for you now.

When you apply a theme to a day of the week, you are giving that day and
overarching focus. That means when you wake up in the morning you won't
have to ask yourself the open ended and difficult question of "What am I going
to do today?" Instead you will need to ask yourself two questions. But the
answers to those questions are far more binary. Here are those two questions:

```

```
The answers to those two questions will direct your attention towards what
you should be concentrating on for that day. Again, this does not mean that
you only do tasks that you've assigned that Daily Theme to throughout the
day. Life doesn't work like that. Any type of Time Theming is designed to
direct your attention to whatever your intention is for that period of time.

When you're ready to dive into Daily Themes further, I encourage you to work
through The Daily Theming Matrix. I explain more around how to theme your
days in that exercise.

### Horizontal Theming

This kind of Time Theming follows the same basic rule of any — you are giving
it an overarching focus to a period of time. But with Horizontal Theming you
are doing so over a period of time across multiple days as opposed to entire
days.

For example, as a writer I know that I can't dedicate an entire day to writing. I
also want to make sure that I write on a consistent basis so I get better at my
craft. So I've created a Horizontal Theme for writing from 9 AM to 11 AM every
weekday. During those two hours my overarching focus is to write. Sometimes
I will write blog posts, sometimes I will write Twitter threads. (Right now I'm
using it to write this guide.) But my overarching focus for those two hours is
writing of some sort.

You may want to incorporate Horizontal Theming into your calendar if you find
that your days are too dynamic for you to theme. You can also use them in
conjunction with Daily Themes, but understand that Horizontal Themes
override Daily Themes. An example of this from my own life is that even
though Thursday is my maintenance day when 9 AM hits, I immediately shift to
writing. Then once my two-hour Horizontal Theme of writing comes to a close,
I go back to those two questions I ask at the start of every day.

A word of caution: do not "hyperschedule" yourself with Horizontal Theme
after Horizontal Theme over the course of your day. You need to leave some
space to breathe. I only have 2-3 Horizontal Themes on my schedule over a 24
hour period. Since you're just getting started, I would start with one.

### Monthly Theming

If you've got goals that you want to bring to life over a 30, 60, or 90 day period

start your day to go along with those two questions I mentioned earlier.

When you have a Monthly Theme in place it further filters the types of tasks
that you're going to do. By doing this, you'll be able to have less friction and
more flow throughout your day.

### Weekly Sprints

The relationship between Weekly Sprints and Monthly Theming is very similar
to the relationship between Horizontal Themes and Daily Themes. Weekly
Sprints override Monthly Themes because they take place within the month
(much like Horizontal Themes take place within the hours of a day). And
Weekly Sprints are designed for things that you want to get done in a shorter
period of time and through consistent focus over that time.

In other words, by the time the week has come to a close your Weekly Sprint
should either be seen through to completion or seen massive progress.

I find that Weekly Sprints are best suited for smaller initiatives that you can
focus on but also need to be done at a breakneck pace. I also find that they
should be used sparingly because that kind of intensity is not sustainable over
the long term.

Weekly Sprints are a little more advanced than any of the other timeframes so I
am hesitant to dive into them any further in this guide. But I have suggested
that you can use The Monthly Theming Matrix to help you decide any Weekly
Sprints you may want to put in your calendar. But, like Horizontal Themes, I
caution you not to overdo it. I run with about six Weekly Sprints over the
course of a year.

I suggest you start slowly with Weekly Sprints and add them to the mix later.
Focus on Monthly Themes first.

### The idea behind Mode-Based Living is a simple one.

Basically, every task on your to-do list requires some sort of modality for you
complete with in a way that keep you moving forward with less friction and
more flow. Those modalities can be categorized into five separate areas:

I've put together a "cheat sheet" for you in this guide that I suggest you refer
to as you start to integrate Mode-Based Living into your workflow. But I'm also
going to explain in more detail what each of these categories mean and how
you can leverage them.

Each of these categories are listed in order of easiest to implement to most
challenging. I'll explain how and when you might want to use each in the words
ahead.

**Time**

Time-based modes are the simplest way to categorize your tasks because you
can quickly identify what you can do in a certain amount of time. For example,
if you have a very tight schedule and only 30 minutes between one meeting
and another, you can look at your to do list and filter out any tasks that will
take you five minutes or less to complete. That way you can get anywhere
from 1 to 6 of those tasks done in that 30 minute time span. This is a far better
option than the default between meetings – which might be checking email, for
example. (And when you do that you will undoubtedly get more tasks added to
your to-do list in the process.)

A simple way to assign time-based modes to your tasks is to choose three
increments of time – short, medium, and long – and use those as ranges of
time. You could choose 5 minutes for short tasks, 15 minutes for medium
tasks, and 30 minutes for longer tasks. Then simply put the number beside any
task that fits that description. I will showcase this in a sample set of tasks
below.


```
MAKE GROCERY LIST (PHONE)
EMAIL MARKETING DEPT. RE: 2022 BUDGET (OUTLOOK)
CALL HEATING COMPANY (PHONE)
ORGANIZE COMPUTER FILES (COMPUTER)
UPDATE 2022 BUDGET (COMPUTER)
CONTACT LIZ ABOUT BOOK PROJECT (OUTLOOK)
```
As you can see I have four tasks that will take me five minutes or less to
complete. I have one task that will take me at least 30 minutes to complete.
And I have another that will take me somewhere in between, approximately 15
minutes.

When you start to filter your tasks through the lens of modes, you can group
them together in a way that allows you to make progress across multiple
projects. That means you don't have to wait on anyone else. And by using
increments of time, you can slot tasks into your day in a more realistic way.

**Resource**

Resource-based modes are basically people, places, and things. You either
need to involve a person (or persons) with a task, be in a certain place to
complete the task, or use a certain thing to complete a task.

Let's look at possible resources in the sample task list that I've already shown
you:

You'll notice that I focussed specifically on things in the above list. I could have
used people instead, particularly the "Marketing Department" or "Liz". You
really only want to use people, places, and things that you come into regular
contact with or use regularly. There's no sense in using the "Heating
Company" as a resource-based mode if you only contact them on an
occasional basis. (You would just mention them in the task itself.)

The way to work through your list by using resource based modes is to group
them in a similar fashion that you would do with time-based modes. So you
would group all of the "Outlook" tasks together so that you could focus on all
the things you needed to do inside of Outlook. The same thing goes for all the
tasks that you would need (or want) to do on your phone.

When you work through your to-do list this way you avoid switching from one
resource to another, which can offset the flow that you want to have as you
work through the list.

This approach works for all of the categories of mode-based living, including
the one I'm going to introduce to you next.


```
MAKE GROCERY LIST (LOW ENERGY)
EMAIL MARKETING DEPT. RE: 2022 BUDGET (HIGH ENERGY)
CALL HEATING COMPANY (LOW ENERGY)
ORGANIZE COMPUTER FILES (HIGH ENERGY)
UPDATE 2022 BUDGET (HIGH ENERGY)
CONTACT LIZ ABOUT BOOK PROJECT (LOW ENERGY)
```
**Energy**

This category is underused and undervalued. Our energy ebbs and flows
throughout the course of the day, depending on our circadian rhythms. If
you're a morning person, then you'll have high energy at the start of your day
and low energy as the day comes to a close. If you're like me, a night owl, the
opposite occurs.

That's why using energy levels as a mode for your to-do list can help you put
your best foot forward with every task on your list.

Let's look at that sample task list again, this time classifying each task with an
energy level.

I would tackle the low energy tasks earlier in the day, grouping them
regardless of time they would take or resources they would require, because I
want to take care of the things that take little bandwidth for me to accomplish.
Then I would move on to the high energy tasks in the afternoon because I'm in
the best position to deliver quality results with them.

You may have noticed that sending an email about the budget is something
that I would classify as a high energy task. That's because I'm not a fan of
doing anything related to money. So while some emails would be considered
low energy for me, that particular email is not. This is something to keep in
mind when you start using energy-based modes with your to do list. Not all
tasks are created equal, especially when it comes to the energy you need to
do them well.

**Activity**

The key thing to know about this category is that you are basically assigning
verbs to your tasks. Sure, you should really begin every task on your to do list
with a verb in order to help your brain understand what it needs to do, but
using activity-based modes can lend a helping hand as well.

You'll want to figure out what types of activities you do on a regular basis and
only use those as modes. Much like with resources, there's no sense in
creating a mode for an activity that you do only every once in a while.

There's a lot of nuance with activity-based modes as well. For example, one of
my primary activities is writing. But not all writing is the same kind of writing.
There's blogging. There's book-writing. There's copywriting. With activity-
based modes I can get more nuanced with the types of writing tasks that I
want to do and group them accordingly. You may find that you can do the
same with an activity that you do regularly as well.

### The 5 Categories of Mode-Based Living

The following is a quick reference guide on the 5 categories of mode-based
living you can apply to tasks on any sort of to-do list. Categorizing your tasks
by mode is one of the elements of TimeCrafting.

**Time-Based Modes**
This category is probably the easiest to start with (or fall back on) because it is
the most easily measured. Having a “5 Minute Mode” is especially useful when
you know you only have a half-hour be- tween appointments because instead
of scanning your to do list looking for tasks to try to do in that timespan, you
can simply do quick math to try to complete six 5 minute tasks instead.
Examples: 5 Minute Mode, 15 Minute Mode, 30 Minute Mode

**Resource-Based Modes**
If you want to know what you need to have at your disposal (or where you
need to be or who you need to talk to about something) to complete a task,
then Resource-Based modes are going to be very helpful. If you use Microsoft
Word a lot, then having “Word Mode” can gather all of your Word-based tasks
into one mode. Same goes for Evernote. You can even create one for the
biggest time suck of all: email.
Examples: Email Mode, Assistant Mode, Kitchen Mode

**Energy-Based Modes**
Your energy levels play a much bigger role in your task and time management
that you give them credit for. By using modes that identify what level of energy
you need to tackle a task, you’re setting yourself up for better results on all of
the items on your to-do list - and you can even be more effective when you not
feeling 100% to boot!
Examples: High Energy Mode, Low Energy Mode, Deep Work Mode

**Activity-Based Modes**
Pretty much any verb is an Activity-Based mode, but you’ll want to use ones
that pertain to your life in a way that you can connect with them more
efficiently and effectively. That means you want to get specific and make them
deeply personal.
Examples: Errand-Running Mode, Reading Mode, Learning Mode

**Theme-Based Modes**
These modes are broad in scope and can be used for those who don’t feel that
Time Theming can work in an ideal way for them because of their schedule.
Essentially they map out well with (or even mirror) your Daily Themes.
Examples: Family Mode, Optimization Mode, Household Mode

Journalling is one of the most undervalued and under used productivity and
time management tools that we have at our disposal. If you take time every
single day to reflect, you can start to see patterns that emerge and can easily
course correct when your relationship with time is starting to falter.

That's a key point: I'm not a huge believer in managing time. Instead, I prefer to
focus on developing a good relationship with time. And journalling helps me do
that.

Chances are, like theming your time and (to a degree) working through your
to-do list based on modes, that you are already journalling.

If you're posting to social media, you're keeping a journal. Taking time out of
your day to share a Facebook post or a photo on Instagram are examples of
keeping a journal. But when you keep a journal on any of those platforms,
you're not journalling for yourself. You're journalling for the world.

It's also likely that you're not painting an accurate picture of what's going on in
your life. There are optics involved. You will pull punches. You won't say what
needs to be said or share what needs to be shared in order for you to develop
as a person.

When you keep a journal for yourself you can be your purest form of yourself.

You can share things that you would never want anyone to read. I speak from
experience. I can tell you that there are things in my journal that I don't
necessarily want anyone to see until I'm no longer able to craft time any
longer. (Because I will be out of time on this planet!)

But by sharing these things in my journal I am learning from them. I am able to
cope with them (and time) better. I am able to take greater command of my
emotions and my responses when those emotions try to take hold. I am able to
start and end my day with a clear and focussed mind.

Journalling is such an integral part of TimeCrafting that I suggested you could
read this section before any other if you wanted to. When you really lean into
journalling you are going to get better at capturing your ideas, your insights,
and your intentions. Practicing this will improve your productivity and your
relationship with time.


I'm not going to tell you how to journal in this guide. It's a very personal
experience and process. I've journalled in a variety of fashions over the years
and no matter which one I choose, I always find something beneficial comes
from it.

But I will share with you a few things to keep in mind that may help you adopt
and stick with the journalling habit:

**Journal to start or end your day**

If you are new to the practice of journalling, I encourage you to tether it to an
existing evening or morning routine. That way it has a greater chance to take
hold as an ongoing practice.

I'm a fan of writing in my journal at the end of the day. I use the practice as a
chronicle of my day and also as a way to empty my mind before I go to bed. It
also serves as a way to forge plans for the days ahead. I think journalling to
close out your day is a smart move – especially if you're a night owl like me.

I know some people like to start off the day with a journal entry. That's another
way to go, especially if you're a morning person. Make sure that you attach it
to your existing morning routine – perhaps as the last item in that routine – so
that it becomes something that happens every day.

Interstitial journalling throughout the day is something that I've practiced as
well. If you're going to practice this then I encourage you to do so in the same
place consistently. That could be in an application or a specific notebook set
aside for journalling. Even if your interstitial journalling doesn't start in one
place it's important to gather up everything you've captured and consolidate it
in one place. The compounding entries in your journal is what will give it
greater power.

**Read your journal**

Writing in your journal on a regular – even daily – basis isn't enough to make it
worthwhile. You need to go back and read it on a regular basis.

I set aside time every week (during my planning session) to read my daily
journal. If I happen to miss a week, I just go back that much further in my
journal. But if I didn't go back and read those entries I wouldn't be able to see
any kind of patterns emerging.

I suppose you could say that by going back to read journal entries you get to
live life three times. And in that third time you can gain a whole lot of insight as
to what you need to do – or maintain — to make your future better.


**Remove friction from the practice**

If you've tried journalling before and it hasn't stuck or you've been hesitant to
start because you feel as if you don't have time to do it justice, there are ways
to remove friction from the journalling practice.

The first is following the first tip I shared with you – attaching your journal-
keeping to a routine that already exists. It will have a much greater chance to
happen if you do that.

Another is to dictate your journal entries into a device of some sort. I actually
dictate more of my journal entries than I physically write or type. When I
dictate my journal entry it removes the friction at the end of the day – friction
that might compromise completing an entry. Once I'm done dictating, I copy
and paste the text into my journalling app of choice and I'm done for the day.

The third thing you can do is to use the photos you've taken on your phone
over the course of the day as a springboard for whatever your journal entry will
be for that day. I've come across many people who struggle with coming up
with something to write about in their journal. But if you take a look at the
photos in your phone, I'm certain you'll find things that you can speak further
on in your journal for that day.

Journalling is a very personal practice. Everyone does it a little bit differently,
taking cues from common practices while adapting others and adding their
own unique twist along the way.

I'm not here to tell you how to journal. I'm just here to tell you that if you do
journal you will be all the better for it.
