# The power of 15 minutes (and other ideas)

_In the return of an occasional series, here are three ideas that have made a significant difference to me recently. (If you detect a theme here about writing and its struggles, well, yes, a book deadline will have that effect on a person… Normal service resumes next time.)_

**The power of fifteen minutes:** via [Paul Bloom’s newsletter](https://smallpotatoes.paulbloom.net/), this old essay, [‘Learning to Work’](https://static1.squarespace.com/static/5b3a3c2596e76feeba40905e/t/5b46366570a6add65490e050/1531328102222/1977workingItOut.pdf) by the psycholinguist Virginia Valian, is superb. Valian describes finding herself unable to work on her PhD, because she was paralysed by anxiety, and the solution she eventually found, which began with resolving to work on her thesis, daily, for just 15 minutes. Working on intimidating projects in tiny increments isn’t brand new advice, of course. But Valian’s account of how she alighted on that length of time is a revelation. She wasn’t trying to _power through_ her anxiety or _attack_ it or _destroy_ it. She wasn’t fighting the war of art. (Perhaps it was no coincidence that her life was focused on anti-war politics at the time.) Instead, she seems to have accepted its presence entirely. In a sense, she conceded its power. And then, by asking herself how much she was actually _willing_ to do on a daily basis, she perceived that her anxiety’s dominion had its limits. It definitely wouldn’t let her work for two hours. But it had no interest in trying to stop her working for a paltry 15 minutes. So she set up her camp on that 15-minute terrain, and from there, she was on her way.

And remember: while you might choose to use a technique like this to build up to 20 minutes, then half an hour, then an hour, and so on, it’s also the case, from one perspective, that life is just one long sequence of 15-minute periods, stacked next to each other. In other words: you never have to do _anything_ for more than 15 minutes! You may just sometimes have to string a few of them end-to-end.

**Writing without stopping:** I’ve used some version of freewriting for years, in the form of [morning pages](https://www.oliverburkeman.com/morningpages). But if I’m honest, I’ve tended to think of this sort of technique as the domain of amateurs, and thus as having no place in my “real” writing routines. That’s changed since reading [_Deep Freewriting_](https://www.amazon.com/Deep-Freewriting-Masterfully-Navigate-Creative-ebook/dp/B084BYL1P1/) by Stephen Lloyd Webber. (My response to your question about his name is: I have no idea!) 

Freewriting often gets presented as a matter of “clearing out the cobwebs”, which intrinsically defines the writing that results from it as worthless. Other times, the idea seems to be that you should cover as many pages as possible, at breakneck speed, in the desperate hope that a few sentences might prove usable, if only by chance. By contrast, “deep freewriting” describes a practice in which you set a timer, then commit to _writing without stopping_ _or deleting_until it goes off, while staying mentally present with the process. Importantly, that needn’t mean writing fast, and could even mean writing very slowly – just as it’s possible to run very slowly yet still be running, as opposed to walking or standing still.

I’ve found this a remarkably useful practice in the early stages of drafting, because it reverses the usual order of things. Instead of holding back from writing until you know what you want to say – which leads to a vicious circle of stopping, deleting, and rewriting everything a hundred times – the commitment to keeping moving means you just write stuff. Whereupon, fairly often, you discover that parts of it contain things you wanted to say. 

Stephen Lloyd Webber recommends this practice for those whose work isn’t focused on writing, too, as a spur to problem-solving and idea-generating. (He also writes about an extraordinary-sounding 24-hour freewriting marathon he once conducted alongside a friend, which is something I want to try.) And if the whole idea of freewriting makes you shudder with its loosey-gooseyness, its incompatibility with your self-image as someone who Just Gets Down to Work™? Believe me, I know exactly what you mean. But that’s a sign that you definitely ought to try it.

**Are you resource-maximising when you should be living?** In his excellent book [_The Uncontrollability of the World_](https://www.wiley.com/en-us/The+Uncontrollability+of+the+World-p-9781509543168), the German social theorist Hartmut Rosa makes a subtle but crucial point about the good life: we’re prone to thinking of it as a matter of _maximising our resources_, when really it isn’t that at all. He’s not merely repeating the timeworn argument that money and possessions won’t make you happy. Rather, his point is that any attempt at a meaningful existence is undermined if your main focus is always on increasing your access to things – even if the “things” in question are wonderful experiences, deep relationships, and so forth, as opposed to sports cars and jewelery. 

This helped me see what rubs me the wrong way about a whole crop of recent “happiness” books: they’re written as if a meaningful life _consists in_ spending it expanding your circle of friends, improving your equanimity through meditation, learning the skills to become more creative, and so forth. Nope. A circle of friends, a stable mind and better creative skills are all very useful for a happy life! But happiness consists in taking pleasure from time spent with those friends; from relishing the absence of anxiety that comes with a calmer mind; or from putting those creative skills to use – from _cashing in_ the resources, you might almost say, rather than from acquiring them. Don’t spend so much of your life amassing the ingredients for enjoying it that you forget to enjoy it.
