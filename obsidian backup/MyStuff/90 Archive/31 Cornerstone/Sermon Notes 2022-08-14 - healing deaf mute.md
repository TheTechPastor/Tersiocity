**To Speak or Not to Speak**

(Mark 7:31–37)

These verses might be introduced with a riddle: Who is permitted to speak but not able, and able to speak but not permitted? That enigmatic question finds its answer in this dramatic account.

After more than a year of public ministry in Galilee, Jesus took His disciples away for a time of isolated instruction. The Twelve had recently affirmed that He was the Son of God (Matt. 14:33) who alone spoke words of eternal life (John 6:68). The time had come for the Lord Jesus to focus His efforts more intently on preparing them for their ministry after His death and resurrection. They would be commissioned as the first generation of gospel preachers, charged with launching the truth toward the ends of the earth (cf. Matt. 28:19; Acts 1:8).

Jesus and His disciples journeyed first to the northwest, traveling outside of Israel into the region of Tyre (modern Lebanon). There they encountered a Gentile woman who desperately sought Jesus’ help and, in the process, exhibited genuine faith in Him (cf. 7:24–30). Her humble persistence provided the apostles with a vivid illustration of their future missionary work. It would not be long before they would see many Gentiles similarly displaying faith in Christ as the gospel spread beyond the borders of Israel (cf. Acts 10:11–48; 11:1–18, 20–25).

After their stay near Tyre ended, Jesus and the Twelve **went out from the region of Tyre, and came through Sidon to the Sea of Galilee, within the region of Decapolis.** Traveling in a circuitous route, in order to extend the time with His disciples, the Lord continued going north through the city of Sidon (situated on the Mediterranean coast about twenty miles from Tyre) before journeying east and then south to His destination on the southeastern shores of the Sea of Galilee.

The **region of Decapolis,** located on the southeast side of the lake, was home to Gentiles and outside the territory of Herod Antipas. The region encompassed ten city-states (the name **Decapolis,** from the Greek _deka_ [“ten”] and _polis_ [“city”], literally means “ten cities”). Archaeological discoveries indicate that these towns were centers of Greek paganism, littered with idols honoring pagan deities like Zeus, Aphrodite, Artemis, and Dionysus. Though the nation of Israel was still Jesus’ priority, His willingness to minister in this Gentile area, much like His interaction with the woman from Tyre, previewed the fact that the gospel was always intended to be preached throughout the whole world. (For more on this point, see chapter 26 in the current volume.) By traveling to Decapolis, Jesus returned to the vicinity of Gerasa where He had earlier healed a man possessed by a legion of demons (cf. Mark 5:1–20). Through the witness of that man (v. 20), along with others from Decapolis who had traveled to Galilee to see Jesus (cf. Matt. 4:25), the news about Jesus had already spread to this area.

The Lord’s time of instruction with the Twelve was ended as massive crowds gathered around Him once again. Matthew 15:29–31 sets the scene:

Jesus went along by the Sea of Galilee, and having gone up on the mountain, He was sitting there. And large crowds came to Him, bringing with them those who were lame, crippled, blind, mute, and many others, and they laid them down at His feet; and He healed them. So the crowd marveled as they saw the mute speaking, the crippled restored, and the lame walking, and the blind seeing; and they glorified the God of Israel.

Though the residents of Decapolis were idol worshipers, they had heard about Jesus’ power and knew He could do what their pagan deities had never done. Consequently, they rushed those who were physically disabled to Him, and He healed them, immediately and completely. Predictably, they “marveled” (from the Greek _thaumazō,_ meaning to be struck with awe) and began to glorify the true God. Ironically, the Jewish leaders of Israel who saw the same miracles rejected Jesus, accusing Him of operating by Satan’s power (Mark 3:22); the Gentile pagans of Decapolis recognized that His power came from God. For the moment, turning from their idols, they offered praise to the God of Israel.

It is in that context that the account described in this passage (Mark 7:31–37) took place. Whereas the parallel passage in Matthew 15:29–31 provides an overview of Jesus’ healings, Mark is the only gospel writer who includes this encounter. Initially, the deaf man described here was unable to speak. By Christ’s will and power, he was enabled to speak. Finally, when the Lord commanded him to keep quiet, he was unable not to speak.

Unable to Speak (7:32)

Friends or family members **brought** a man **who was deaf and spoke with difficulty** to see Jesus. His deafness was likely congenital or long-term; without being able to hear as a child, he was unable to learn how to speak, thereby resulting in a severe speech impediment. In that world, no remedies existed for such a condition. Those suffering from such physical impairments were ostracized by society. Even in Israel, deaf people, because of their hearing loss and speech defects, were generally regarded as mentally handicapped. Adding insult to injury, the Jews alleged that disabilities like deafness or blindness were the direct result of God’s judgment for sin (cf. John 9:1–2). That this man lived in a pagan society likely meant that the ill treatment and disdain he endured was even worse.

Nonetheless, some cared enough about this man to bring him to Jesus, **and they implored Him to lay His hand on him.** In this context, the verb **implored** (from the Greek verb _parakaleō_) means “to beg” or “to entreat” with a sense of urgency. In desperation they pled on behalf of their friend, who could not speak for himself, that Jesus would enable him to hear. Jesus often laid His hands on people to visually and tangibly demonstrate His power to the sufferers (cf. Mark 1:31, 41; 5:41; 6:5; 8:22, 25). Unlike the Pharisees and scribes, who considered themselves to be above the common people, Jesus gladly mingled with the crowds and willingly extended His touch toward those in need. Doing so displayed His and heaven’s tender compassion and personal care. It also demonstrated that He was not afraid of ceremonial defilement. Jesus was never defiled by those whom He touched—whether it was a leper (1:40–41), a woman with a hemorrhage of blood (5:25–34), a dead body (5:41–42), or a Gentile man who suffered from deafness. Rather than being corrupted by them, they were cleansed and restored by Him.

Enabled to Speak (7:33–35)

Responding with compassion as He always did (cf. Matt. 9:36; 14:14; Mark 1:41; 8:2, etc.), **Jesus took** the man **aside from the crowd, by himself.** In the midst of the pressing throng, with many others waiting to be healed, the Lord Jesus gave His attention to one desperate man who had surely been ignored and neglected throughout his life. For as long as the man could remember, he had been scorned, ostracized, and despised. But in that moment, he received the undivided attention and compassion of the Creator Himself.

In an act of profound kindness, the Lord began to communicate in sign language, using gestures and nonverbal signals. Jesus used four specific signs to make His point. First, He **put His fingers into** both of **his ears** to indicate that He recognized the man’s physical problem. Jesus understood that he was not stunted mentally or possessed by demons, as some may have thought; he simply could not hear. The Lord used a symbolic gesture to demonstrate that He had rightly diagnosed the medical issue. Second, **after spitting, He touched his tongue with the saliva.** Jesus again employed a physical gesture to identify the man’s speech disability. Though Jesus used **saliva** in His healings on two other occasions (cf. Mark 8:23; John 9:6), it obviously had no power. However, ancient people generally believed that saliva had healing properties. The deaf man would have understood that Jesus’ use of saliva meant He intended to heal him. Third, **looking up to heaven,** Jesus demonstrated that the creative power He exercised came from God. Even as a pagan, the man would have understood what Jesus meant by gazing toward heaven. Fourth, by giving **a deep sigh,** the Lord communicated a sincere sympathy for the long agonies of this man’s disability. An honest groaning visibly projected pain and heartache on the man’s behalf. So, using nonverbal communication, the Lord Jesus taught this man about both God’s power and His compassion. The Son of God would heal him, with power that came from above, because He cared deeply about him.

Those two wonderful truths must have filled the man’s heart and mind as the miraculous happened. **Jesus said to him, “Ephphatha!” that is, “Be opened!”** By using the Aramaic term **Ephphatha,** Mark provided an exact quote of Jesus’ words, since the colloquial language He spoke was Aramaic. However, Mark immediately translated it for his Greek-speaking readers: **“Be opened!”** With a command from the incarnate Creator, the man was immediately given new hearing organs and his tongue was miraculously loosed to speak. As Mark explains, **his ears were opened, and the impediment of his tongue was removed, and he began speaking plainly.** The word **impediment,** from the Greek word _desmon_, means “bond” or “chains.” It was as if his speech had been imprisoned in the dungeon of deafness. Immediately, he was set free and able to hear perfectly and speak plainly.

The extent of the miracle went beyond merely repairing the man’s physical faculties. He was also given the ability of miraculous language acquisition. Not only could he hear sounds, he was able to understand and articulate words without needing any linguistic training or speech therapy. The word **plainly** comes from the Greek word _orthōs_, meaning “straight” or “right.” The English medical terms “orthopedics” and “orthodontics” are derived from that Greek term. In an instant, the One who created the world (John 1:1–3), and upholds it with “the word of His power” (Heb. 1:3), supernaturally enabled this man to hear and speak fluently. Like every miracle Jesus performed, this healing was an act of divine creative energy through His word, the same way He made the universe in the beginning (cf. Gen. 1:3, 6, 9, 14, 20, 24, 26).

Unable Not to Speak (7:36–37)

No doubt, the man’s reaction was one of exuberant joy. Naturally, his instant impulse was to tell everyone what happened. But Jesus instructed him and his friends to keep quiet—an immense restraint in light of such an experience. Yet, **He gave them orders not to tell anyone.**

**Orders** (from the Greek _diastellomai_) refers to a command. That Jesus commanded this man to remain quiet may seem strange, not only because He had just given him the ability to speak but also because the Lord had earlier told the Garasene demoniac to do the exact opposite:

He said to him, “Go home to your people and report to them what great things the Lord has done for you, and how He had mercy on you.” And he went away and began to proclaim in Decapolis what great things Jesus had done for him; and everyone was amazed. (Mark 5:19–20)

One might wonder why Jesus instructed the former demoniac to spread the news about Him throughout the region of Decapolis and later told the former deaf man to keep quiet. There was an important difference. The former demoniac was the first missionary to that Gentile area. But now, largely through his witness, the news about Jesus’ miracle-working power was well-known throughout the region, resulting in widespread euphoria. The situation had reached epic proportions due to the unbridled enthusiasm of the unwieldy crowds. As in Galilee, the Lord had no desire to add fuel to the fire of their inherently materialistic and political expectations about Him (cf. John 6:15).

Jesus issued similar commands at other times as well (cf. Matt. 8:4; 9:30; 12:16; 17:9; Mark 1:25, 34, 44; 3:12; 5:43; 7:36; 8:26, 30; 9:9; Luke 4:41; 9:21). On certain occasions, the Lord insisted on silence because He knew the report would amplify the enthusiastic fervor of the crowds, which would only hinder His ministry (cf. Mark 1:40–45; John 6:14–15). As noted above, that was likely part of Jesus’ concern on this occasion since large crowds were already flocking to Him in the Decapolis (cf. Mark 8:1–10). At other times, the gag order served as an act of judgment on unbelievers by obscuring the truth from those who had permanently rejected Him (cf. Luke 9:21).

However, the primary reason Jesus repeatedly insisted on this kind of silence is found in Mark 8:30–31. After His disciples identified Him as the Messiah and the Son of God (v. 29; cf. Matt. 16:18), “He warned them to tell no one about Him. And He began to teach them that the Son of Man must suffer many things and be rejected by the elders and the chief priests and the scribes, and be killed, and after three days rise again.” Knowing that His earthly mission would not be accomplished until after His death and resurrection, Jesus instructed even His own disciples to remain quiet until after the story was complete. Many whom He healed knew Him merely as a miracle worker, but Jesus had come for a far more glorious purpose (cf. Luke 19:10). A message that highlighted only His miraculous healings would be inadequate. The full message about Him must include the truth that “Christ died for our sins according to the Scriptures, and that He was buried, and that He was raised on the third day according to the Scriptures” (1 Cor. 15:3–4).

Aware of their uncontainable elation, the Lord repeated His instruction, **but the more He ordered them, the more widely they continued to proclaim it.** In spite of His repeated commands, the man and his friends, unable to contain their joy, proved disobedient. Ironically, though Jesus had healed his ears, the man refused to listen to the Lord’s instruction. It is likely that Jesus’ admonition was also aimed at bystanders in the crowd who had witnessed this stunning miracle. The disciples must have wondered also why Jesus would issue such a command. Even they would only later come to understand the full story of Jesus’ work—including His death and resurrection (cf. Mark 9:32; Luke 9:45; 18:34; John 12:16).

Upon seeing the miraculous wonders He did, including the transformation of this deaf man, the people in the crowd **were utterly astonished.** The word **utterly** comes from the Greek term _huperperissōs_, meaning “exceedingly,” “above all measure,” or “superabundantly.” **Astonished** translates a form of the word _ekplessō_, which means “to be struck with amazement” or, colloquially, “to be blown out of one’s mind.” The people were completely awestruck and unable to contain themselves. So, in spite of Jesus’ instruction to the contrary, they too spread the word everywhere.

In their excitement, the people exclaimed, **He has done all things well. He makes even the deaf to hear and the mute to speak.** The adverb **well** translates the Greek word _kalōs,_ meaning “rightly,” “correctly,” or “appropriately.” The people were commenting on the perfection of His miracles. He made the blind to see, the lame to walk, **the deaf to hear and the mute to speak.** Their recovery was immediate, and their restoration complete. Jesus’ healings never failed; they were perfect every time.

The word **mute** comes from the Greek word _alalos_, meaning “without speech.” That term is used only three times in the Gospels, all in Mark (cf. 7:37; 9:17, 25). Earlier, in verse 32, Mark used an even less common word to describe this man’s condition. The phrase “spoke with difficulty” translates a form of the Greek word _mogilalos_, occurring only here in the New Testament. Significantly, that same word occurs only once in the Septuagint (the ancient Greek translation of the Old Testament) in Isaiah 35. That prophetic passage describes the wonders of the future millennial kingdom when Christ returns to reign on the earth: the desert will blossom with beautiful flowers (vv. 1–2), Israel and the surrounding nations will see the glory of the Lord God (v. 2), the weak and feeble will be encouraged (v. 3), and God’s enemies will be judged and the righteous saved (v. 4). In that context, Isaiah writes, “Then the eyes of the blind will be opened and the ears of the deaf will be unstopped. Then the lame will leap like a deer, and the tongue of the mute will shout for joy” (vv. 5–6). The word “mute” (from the Hebrew word ‘_illem_) is translated by a form of the Greek word _mogilalos_ in the Septuagint. By using that same rare term, Mark connected his account with the prophecy of Isaiah 35. The healings Jesus performed, like the curing of a deaf man with a severe speech impediment, were previews of the glories of His future messianic kingdom, when death and disease will be greatly diminished (cf. Isa. 29:18; 30:23; 32:14–15; 65:20).

Isaiah 35:8–10 continues its description of the millennial kingdom with a beautiful picture of the redeemed who will dwell there:

A highway will be there, a roadway,

And it will be called the Highway of Holiness.

The unclean will not travel on it,

But it will be for him who walks that way,

And fools will not wander on it.

No lion will be there,

Nor will any vicious beast go up on it;

These will not be found there.

But the redeemed will walk there,

And the ransomed of the Lord will return

And come with joyful shouting to Zion,

With everlasting joy upon their heads.

They will find gladness and joy,

And sorrow and sighing will flee away.

While the people Jesus healed physically during His ministry were right to rejoice, their momentary exuberance cannot compare to the eternal joys that await those whom He has saved spiritually and to whom He has promised eternal glorified bodies (cf. John 11:25–26; 1 Cor. 15:20–28, 35–56). During the millennial kingdom (cf. Rev. 20:1–6) and then forever on the new earth (cf. Rev. 21:1–22:5), the redeemed will exult in the wonder of their completed salvation.

By healing temporal maladies, the Lord Jesus pointed people to something greater: the hope of eternal life (cf. John 5:40; 6:35; 10:10; 17:2–3). Through Him, forgiveness from sin and reconciliation with God are readily available to everyone who believes the gospel, whether Jew or Gentile (cf. Rom. 1:16; 2 Cor. 5:20–21; Gal. 3:28). Jesus is infinitely more than a miracle worker and the greatest teacher. He is the only Savior (John 14:6; Acts 4:12) who died to pay the penalty for sin (cf. Isa. 53:4–5; Rom. 4:25; Col. 2:13–14; 1 Peter 3:18) and rose victoriously to demonstrate His power over death (cf. Acts 2:24; 17:31; Rom. 8:11; 1 Cor. 15:20–22, 54–56). Those who repent and believe in Him savingly will experience His life-giving power for all of eternity (cf. John 4:14; 7:38; Rev. 7:17; 21:6). Spiritually, their sinful hearts are cleansed at the moment of conversion (cf. Acts 10:43; 15:9; Rom. 8:1; 2 Cor. 5:17; Titus 3:4–7). Physically, their bodies will one day be resurrected, never to experience disease or decay again (cf. John 5:28–29; 1 Cor. 15:42–56; 2 Cor. 5:1–4; Rev. 21:4). In that state of glorified perfection, free from both sin and sickness, they will forever worship their Redeemer and King (cf. Rev. 5:13; 19:1–6; 22:3–4).

V. The deaf man healed in Decapolis, **31–37**. He left the region pertaining to Tyre, because the news of this healing would bring other applicants, and friend and foe in Galilee would soon learn where he was. **31. Borders**, as in ver. 24. King James’ version here has “coasts,” from the passion for varying the translation. Leaving southern Phœnicia our Lord does not return across Galilee to Capernaum, but goes northward **through Sidon**. This is the correct Greek text, and it throws light on the Lord’s movements at this juncture. He is keeping away from the dominions of Herod Antipas. From Tyre to Sidon is about twenty miles. From northern Phœnicia he goes eastward above Galilee into the dominions of Philip the tetrarch (Luke 3:1), which included all the country immediately east of the upper Jordan and the Sea of Galilee. Then turning southward he goes down to Decapolis, southeast of the Sea of Galilee. Philip was a much more mild and just ruler than his brother Herod Antipas, and had no special cause of apprehension such as the slayer of John the Baptist felt. **Decapolis**, as in 5:20, was a region of ten associated towns, which had certain privileges in common. Matthew says (15:29) “into the mountain,” _i. e_., the mountain range that runs all along east of the Jordan and of its lakes.

**32. One that was deaf**. Matthew here tells (15:30, 31) of his healing very many “lame, blind, dumb, maimed, and many others.” Mark mentions only one case, and describes that in his usual vivid way. **Had an impediment in his speech**, literally “speaking with difficulty.” **33. Took him aside … privately**. He often healed in the presence of others, and the reason for this privacy is not plain. It may have been (comp. ver. 36) to diminish the popular excitement which these miraculous cures were causing. Symbolic actions, touching the diseased organ, etc., were performed in various other cases, but not in all. The manner and circumstances of his healing were immensely varied. **34. Sighed**, a strong term, more exactly expressed by “groaned,” viz., in sympathy for suffering. So do we sometimes sigh or groan over want or other suffering we are about to relieve. **Ephphatha**. We had a similar case of Mark’s giving the Aramaic words used by Jesus in 5:41. This a peculiarity of Mark’s Gospel.

**36. Charged them**. (comp. 3:12; 5:43.) In this same region of Decapolis, Jesus had directed the man delivered from the legion of demons to tell about it (5:20). Probably this with the news of many cures in Galilee, and the wonderful story of feeding the five thousand, a few miles north, had wrought great excitement, as shown by the multitude now assembled; and so there would be danger of fanaticism, as frequently in Galilee. **So much the more**, as in 5:43. **37. The deaf** and **the dumb** are plural. Mark has described only a single case, but this plural is explained by Matt. 15:30, 31.

**31** None of the other evangelists record this story. Mark includes it because it gives an account of another healing on Gentile territory, and this is its connection with the preceding story. The geographic references are difficult. Jesus journeys north from Tyre through Sidon and then apparently in a southeasterly direction through the territory of Herod Philip to the eastern side of the Sea of Galilee and into the territory of the Decapolis. The textual tradition indicates that the copyists had problems with this circuitous journey (cf. Notes). Yet the journey is not geographically impossible. Mark gives us no information as to what Jesus was doing during the journey. The Decapolis (the territory of the ten Greek cities) was largely Gentile, but there were also a significant number of Jews living there.

**32** Mark says that the man brought to Jesus was “deaf and could hardly talk” (NEB has “had an impediment in his speech”). The adjective is _mogilalos_ and is far from being a common word. This is its only occurrence in the entire NT; and it appears only once in the LXX, where its use is of great importance to the meaning of this story. Certainly Mark must have had Isaiah 35:6 in mind, which is a poetical description of the Messianic Age: “Then will the lame leap like a deer, and the tongue of the dumb [_mogilalos_] shout for joy.” (For the rabbinic understanding of this text as fulfilled in the age of the Messiah, see R _Genesis_ 95; Midrash _Tehillim_ 146.8.)

The people brought the deaf mute to Jesus and begged him to lay his hands on him. Although they did not explicitly say so, they obviously wanted Jesus to heal the man. “They begged” (_parakalousin_) shows their concern for him, and Jesus responds to it. The deaf mute could make no intelligible request for himself.

**33** In order to deal more personally with the mute, Jesus took him apart from the crowd. Mark emphasizes Jesus’ desire to have personal contact with the people he heals. Here his actions seem to be done to help the man exercise faith—the fingers placed in his ears apparently indicate they were to be unblocked and the saliva on the tongue indicates it was going to be restored to normal use. Calvin (2:271–72) comments

The laying on of hands would of itself have been sufficiently efficacious, and even, without moving a finger, he might have accomplished it by a single act of his will, but it is evident that he made abundant use of outward signs, when they were found to be advantageous. Thus, by touching the tongue with spittle, he intended to point out that the faculty of speech was communicated by himself alone; and by putting his fingers into the ears, he showed that it belonged to his office to pierce the ears of the deaf.

(See also SBK, 2:17, where a similar understanding of the use of the fingers and spittle is offered.)

**34–35** Jesus’ looking up to heaven (v. 34) is best understood as an attitude of prayer (cf. John 11:41; 17:1), and perhaps it was also a way of showing the man that God was the source of his power. The “deep sigh” should be interpreted together with the looking up into heaven. It was

the “sighing” of the prayer, the sighing that accompanied the concealed communion of Jesus with the Father. If this looking up and sighing had been permanent technical aspects of the modus operandi of Jesus, we should definitely find references to them in early Christianity, but what we find there is that it is repeatedly stated that healing was simply performed only “in the name of Jesus”! (Van der Loos, _Miracles of Jesus_, p. 327)

Jesus’ prayer consisted of only a single word _Ephphatha_. According to his custom, Mark explains the meaning of this Aramaic word to his readers: “Be opened!” When Jesus spoke, as in this case, to the peasant people of Palestine, he used Aramaic (cf. Mark 5:41). To the Syrophoenician woman, he almost certainly spoke Greek.

The effect of the command was instantaneous (v. 35). The man’s ears were opened, his tongue loosed, and he spoke “plainly” (_orthōs_). He had not been completely mute; he had a speech impediment in his voice “could hardly talk” (v. 32).

**36–37** Jesus again ordered the crowd not to divulge the miracle, but this had no effect at all (v. 36). The more he insisted on their not talking about it, the more they blazed the miracle abroad. This is very much in contrast to the attitude of the crowd when, also in the Decapolis, he healed the Gadarene demoniac. Then the crowd was afraid and wanted to get rid of Jesus. His reason for enjoining silence here was probably the same as in 1:44. He did not want a false concept of him as only a miracle worker to spread lest it touch off a messianic insurrection and prevent him from accomplishing his God-appointed mission. Another result of the healing was that the people were “overwhelmed” (_hyperperissōs_, a true _hapax legomenon_—i.e., a word occurring only once in Greek literature—that means “beyond measure,” “in the extreme”). The statement “he has done everything well” reminds us of Genesis 1:31: “God saw all that he made, and it was very good.” The reminder is not unsuitable, for in a profound sense Jesus’ work is indeed “a new creation.” And again we are reminded of the messianic significance of this miracle by words that reflect Isaiah 35:5–6 (already anticipated by the use of _mogilalon_[“hardly speak”] in 7:32):

Then will the eyes of the blind be opened and the ears of the deaf unstopped. Then will the lame leap like a deer, and the mute tongue shout for joy.

There can be no doubt that for Mark the significance of this miracle was the proclamation of the gospel in the territory of the Gentiles, a sign of the messianic activity of Jesus.

Healing a Deaf Man

_And again, departing from the region of Tyre and Sidon, He came through the midst of the region of Decapolis to the Sea of Galilee._ _32_ _Then they brought to Him one who was deaf and had an impediment in his speech, and they begged Him to put His hand on him._ _33_ _And He took him aside from the multitude, and put His fingers in his ears, and He spat and touched his tongue._ _34_ _Then, looking up to heaven, He sighed, and said to him, “Ephphatha,” that is, “Be opened.”_ _35_ _Immediately his ears were opened, and the impediment of his tongue was loosed, and he spoke plainly._ _36_ _Then He commanded them that they should tell no one; but the more He commanded them, the more widely they proclaimed it._ _37_ _And they were astonished beyond measure, saying, “He has done all things well. He makes both the deaf to hear and the mute to speak” (Mark 7:31–37)._

**T**his miracle, recorded only by Mark, occurred as Christ returned from Tyre and Sidon where he had granted the request of the Syro-Phoenician woman by healing her daughter. It was a long, circuitous route that he traveled from the Mediterranean to Decapolis. Decapolis, lying south and east of Galilee, began on the west side of the Jordan and stretched across that river to the east, encompassing the territory of Manasseh (Numbers 32:33–42). As the name _Decapolis_ indicates, originally the area contained ten cities, most of them having been built by followers of Alexander the great and rebuilt by the Romans. Near the beginning of his ministry, it was to Decapolis that Jesus went after he had cast demons out of two men and into a herd of swine in Gadara (Mark 5:20), and multitudes from Decapolis followed the Lord (Matthew 4:25).

1. The Plight of Mankind-Deafness: Mark 7:31–32

The deafness of the man brought to Jesus pictures the spiritual condition of all humankind (Acts 28:27). How? Because, as illustrated in this case, the deafness had serious ramifications in three areas.

_A. Isolation_

Unlike the blind person who, although also isolated, could be communicated with, it was nearly impossible to communicate with a deaf person. Oral communication was impossible; written communication was slow and laborious; sign language was crude and inadequate. So a deaf person remained isolated from much in life in those times of no mass media and no science of lip reading or sign language.

Similarly, the unsaved person is deaf to God’s word, though he has the ability to see God’s revelation in nature (Psalm 19:1–6). But God’s word is to him foolishness (1 Corinthians 2:14).

_B. Ignorance_

Since he lived in a day before mass media communication, including books, this deaf man must have known very little about most areas of life. He would have known only what he could learn through sight. No television, no newspaper, no magazines, no encyclopedias, which today feed our minds, were available to him to read and thus to compensate for what he could not hear. He lived in intellectual darkness (Ephesians 4:18).

_C. Inability_

In addition to being deaf, the man also had difficulty in speaking. Apparently, he could speak only in a stammering and unintelligible voice. Though not totally mute, he was practically so, for only when he was healed was he able to use his organs of speech properly (35). Similarly, the unsaved man cannot communicate with God. He makes sounds about religious interests, but that is all.

2. The Power of the Master—Deliverance: Mark 7:33–37

_A. The Method of Deliverance—Contrary (7:33)_

Though the man’s friends had specified the method they wanted the Lord to use (to lay his hands on him), Jesus did not tie himself to their wishes or to any one method. First, he separated the man from the crowd so as not to cater to their persistent desire to see a sign. Then he put his fingers into the man’s ears and placed his own saliva on his tongue. Christ probably used this different method in order to show clearly that the power to heal proceeded from his person, and to encourage the man who could not hear anything that Christ might have spoken or prayed.

The lesson is clear: a method, however successful, may be inappropriate in some instances. We must deal with people as individuals, meeting their needs with a variety of methods.

_B. The Motivation of Deliverance—Compassion (7:34)_

Our Lord gave a deep sigh. Luther is reported to have said, “This sigh was not drawn from Christ on account of the single tongue and ear of this poor man, but is a common sigh over all tongues and ears, yea over all hearts, bodies, and souls, and over all men from Adam to his last descendant.” The next chapter records another occasion when Christ sighed over the people’s desire to have a sign (Mark 8:12 where the verb is intensified). Sin causes believers to sigh today also, for we groan while waiting for the redemption of our bodies (Romans 8:23). Life in our present bodies produces groans (2 Corinthians 5:2, 4); disobedient church members cause their leaders to groan (Hebrews 13:17); and sin sometimes causes brothers to murmur or groan against each other (James 5:9). Sin and sighing go hand in hand.

As he groaned, the Lord looked up into heaven. Though the deaf man could not hear the sigh, he could see the Lord looking up—a sight that must have encouraged his faith. Then, in Aramaic, the Lord said, “Be opened.”

_C. The Manner of Deliverance—Complete (7:35–37)_

The cure was complete, for the man spoke in a right manner and his hearing was restored. But the Lord commanded that no one be told. Publicity would only impede his mission at that point. But the more he insisted, the more the crowd spread the news. They acknowledged that Christ did all things well, an echo of Genesis chapter 1, and that he was fulfilling the prophecy of Isaiah 53:5–6. In other words, knowingly or unknowingly, the crowd was acknowledging that Jesus of Nazareth was the creator-messiah.

Today he is still doing all things well, even in this scene of sin (Romans 8:28). And one day he will reign over the earth and bring righteousness, healing, peace, and prosperity.

Healing A Deaf And Dumb Man

Mark 7:31–37

The Gospel of Matthew reports that when Christ returned to Palestine from a trip to Phoenicia, “great multitudes came unto him, having with them those that were lame, blind, dumb, maimed, and many others, and cast them down at Jesus’ feet, and he healed them” (Matthew 15:30). The Gospel of Mark selects one of those cases of healing from this occasion and describes it in detail in our text for this chapter. This miracle had to do with the miraculous healing of a deaf and dumb man who was brought to Christ at this time.

To study this miracle of the healing of a deaf and dumb man by Christ, we will consider the place of the miracle (v. 31), the problem for the miracle (v. 32), the performing of the miracle (vv. 33–35), the precept after the miracle (v. 36), and the praise after the miracle (v. 37).

**A. THE PLACE OF THE MIRACLE**

This miracle of the healing of a deaf and dumb man occurred in the “midst of the coasts [borders] of Decapolis” (v. 31). Here we will look at the departure for the place and the description of the place.

**1. The Departure for the Place**

“And again, departing from the coasts of Tyre and Sidon [Phoenicia], he [Christ] came unto the sea of Galilee, through the midst of the coasts of Decapolis” (v. 31). The departure from Tyre and Sidon to the place where the miracle occurred, which is recorded in our text in Mark, tells us about the schedule of Christ and the stamina of Christ.

_The schedule of Christ in the departure_. “Again, departing.” These two words remind us that Christ was on a schedule which He kept diligently. It was the schedule God had given Him. And in following God’s schedule, Christ was always where God the Father wanted Him at any given moment. Christ departed from Phoenicia right after He had cast out a demon from a girl (Matthew 15:22–29). Though He experienced success in His ministry in Phoenicia, He did not let this cause Him to forsake His Divine schedule, but He still kept on schedule and headed east and south to the area known as Decapolis.

We see an emphasis on Christ’s commitment to the Divine schedule throughout the Gospels. One case is in John 4. “He left Judea, and departed again into Galilee. And he must needs go through Samaria” (John 4:3, 4). Samaria was on His schedule. He was to encounter a woman at Jacob’s well in Samaria and this encounter would result in her glorious conversion. It was all in the plan of God. Another case is in John 7. Christ was urged to go to Jerusalem to attend a feast, but it was not yet time for Him to go to Jerusalem, so He said, “I go not up yet unto this feast; for my time is not yet full come” (John 7:8). “My time” was His Divinely appointed schedule. And Christ kept to that schedule without deviation.

God’s servants need help here, for too often they get off God’s schedule. Sometimes it is because of discouragement and difficulties. Other times it is because of success, for success can also get men off God’s schedule. But whatever the reason, many are not running according to schedule. Like many planes, trains and buses, their timetable is seldom kept. And as in the case of planes, trains, and buses, this failure is not good for business, namely, the individual’s involvement in the Lord’s business.

_The stamina of Christ for the departure_. The travels of Christ from Phoenicia to Decapolis covered in the neighborhood of 75 miles. This distance is nothing to us today with our fast modes of transportation. But in Christ’s day, such travels were generally done by the feet—that is, Christ walked that distance. Matthew Henry, in commenting about these lengthy travels, said, “Such long walks did our Lord Jesus take, when he went about doing good.” It takes a lot of stamina to stay on God’s schedule for one’s life. You may have to spend a lot of time on the trail walking if you would stay in God’s will and on God’s timetable. Christ stayed right on schedule. He had the spiritual stamina to follow God’s plan consistently though some of the journey was long and bone-wearying. If you lack that all important ingredient of stamina in your spiritual pursuits, get into the Word of God more earnestly to build up your spiritual strength so you can stay on schedule.

**2. The Description of the Place**

As the name indicates, Decapolis was named for an area containing ten cities in the time of Christ. These cities were in league with one another “to promote their common interests in trade and commerce, and for mutual protection against the peoples surrounding them. This particular league seems to have been constituted about the time of Pompey’s campaign in Syria, 65 b.c.… They were independent of the local tetrarchy, and answerable directly to the governor of Syria” (Ewing). This area was mostly in the Trans-Jordan region of Palestine (area east of the Jordan which was called Gilead in the Old Testament times [Numbers 32:1, 26, 29, 39, 40] which included the land which the tribe of Gad and the half tribe of Manasseh chose to live in when the Israelites moved to Canaan in the day of Moses and Joshua). However, one city (Scythopolis) of the ten cities was located on the western side of the Jordan, and another city (Damascus) was located in Syria. Historians vary some regarding the identity of the ten cities. E. M. Blaiklock in _The Zondervan Pictorial Encyclopedia of the Bible_ lists the ten cities as follows: Philadelphia, Gerasa (later called Jerash), Pella, Scythopolis (ancient Bethshean), Gardara, Dion, Kanatha, Hippos, Raphana, and Damascus. Bible maps of the area often give special marking for these ten cities.

Three of the thirty-three miracles of Christ which we are studying occurred in Decapolis. When one considers these three miracles along with all those that come under the summary lists of miracles which were done in Decapolis, one can easily perceive that Christ had a very fruitful ministry in Decapolis. It was not, however, without hostilities. At least once in Decapolis (near the city of Gadara) Christ was asked to leave the area after working a miracle (Mark 5:17). But His work in the Decapolis area was very great and often given very good reception.

**B. THE PROBLEM FOR THE MIRACLE**

“And they bring unto him one that was deaf, and had an impediment in his speech; and they beseech him to put his hand upon him” (v. 32). From this verse regarding the problem for the miracle, we note the particulars of the problem, the presenting of the problem, and the petition about the problem.

**1. The Particulars of the Problem**

Two things were in need of help regarding the man in our text. The man was deaf and dumb.

_Deaf_. The first problem stated in Scripture about this man was that he “was deaf.” The deafness was absolute. He simply could not hear. This would greatly handicap the man especially in those days when sign language and other help in communicating by the deaf did not exist as well as it does today, and medical practices offered no hope at all.

Not only was the man greatly handicapped by this deafness, but he was also greatly imperiled by his deafness. We use our ears more than we realize to avoid harm. Spiritual deafness is no different. When we refuse to hear the Word of God, we imperil our souls greatly, for we do not hear the warnings of Scripture of eternal condemnation. Physical deafness is bad, but spiritual deafness is infinitely worse.

_Dumb_. The man was also dumb in that he “had an impediment in his speech” that was so bad he could not communicate to people with his mouth. The word translated “impediment” does not mean the man could not make any sounds but that he had great difficulty in speaking. Thus this dumbness was not one of _sound_ but one of _sense_. That is, the man could make sounds with his mouth, but he could not make sense with the sounds. He was not able to make himself understood. The sounds he made with his mouth were gibberish. So for all practical purposes he was considered dumb as well as deaf.

This cause of the dumbness was twofold. One cause had to do with his tongue, the other with his ears.

First, the _tongue cause_. The cause of the dumbness which is emphasized in our text is some physical impairment of the tongue which made it impossible for him to speak properly. This problem is indicated by the healing report which said “the string of his tongue was loosed” (v. 35). We would say the man was “tongue-tied.” This problem was obviously genetic, and we have no reason to believe otherwise about his deafness, too. So the man had some big problems. But as we have noted in previous miracles, Christ specializes in solving big problems; and the bigger the problem the more glory Christ receives in solving it.

Second, the _ear cause_. Another cause that would contribute to the man’s dumbness was his inability to hear. Not hearing others speak, he would not know what sort of sounds to make. Deafness and dumbness often go hand in hand. We learn to speak by hearing. When we cannot hear, we will have difficulty speaking. If one becomes deaf late in life, he will, of course, be able to speak much better than one who became deaf early in life or was born deaf. But hearing always has much to do with speaking well.

This relationship of the problem of dumbness with deafness pictures some of the effects of sin upon a man spiritually. Those who turn a deaf ear to the Word of God will have great difficulty speaking properly in spiritual matters. Sin causes man to speak unintelligibly about spiritual matters. “Even the most cultured and educated sinner betrays an impediment in his speech as soon as spiritual truths are introduced” (Lockyer). But when a man opens his ears to receive the Gospel message, his spiritual speech will improve greatly.

**2. The Presenting of the Problem**

The phrase, “they bring unto him” (v. 32) describes the presenting of the problem to Christ by others. From this “presenting,” we note several lessons on service. They are found in the work of this presenting and in the wisdom in this presenting.

_The work of this presenting_. “They” who presented the man to Christ were involved in a work all men need to be involved in, namely, bringing men to Jesus Christ for the Divine solving of their needs. Our text shows us that this work of presenting or bringing one to Christ involves compassion, cost, and capacity.

First, _compassion_. Our text shows us that in bringing people to Christ, one will have to have compassion for the people needing help. Those who brought the deaf and dumb man to Christ had to have compassion for the man in his problems. Without compassion, they would not have brought the man to Christ. It has always been that way.

Second, _cost_. Also needed to bring others to Christ is a willingness to pay the cost. It took much time and effort on the part of those who brought this deaf and dumb man to Christ. Doing this work is not cheap if you want to do it well. Cheap and lazy folk will do little in bringing people to Christ.

Third, _capacity_. Our text indicates that one’s task in bringing folk to Christ may not be one of prestigious nature in the eyes of men, but it may be nothing more than the work of these men in bringing the deaf and dumb man to Christ. The folk in our text did the simple task of taking a man to where Christ was so Christ could heal him. One’s work may simply be to bring a person to church to hear a sermon or to provide money for a missionary to proclaim the Gospel in some far off land to win people to Jesus Christ. All of these efforts are part of bringing people to Christ just as being a pastor or evangelist are efforts to bring men to Christ; but they are not, of course, as prestigious in men’s sight. Make sure you are in some part of the work of bringing folk to the Savior Jesus Christ whether men give status to your task or not.

_The wisdom in this presenting_. “They” brought the man to Christ for help. Thus they knew where to take the needy. This is wisdom indeed. There are many in society who recognize the problems of others. But they do not know the cure. They know someone is sick spiritually and morally, but they have no clue as to the cure. And such people are often unaware of their ignorance, for many today are championing strange remedies for the great needs of men. But whether they are aware of their ignorance or not, the point is they do not take people to Christ for help. The popular thing today is to take folk to some psychologist or psychiatrist for help instead of to Christ. But it is a tragic mistake which only increases the problem rather than solves it. So few people in this world recognize that Jesus Christ is the One Who is the remedy for the needs of the soul of man. Christ is the answer to the greatest problems and needs of men. “He is a rewarder of them that diligently seek him” (Hebrews 11:6).

**3. The Petition About the Problem**

“They beseech him to put his hand upon him.” In this healing, the afflicted man did not petition Christ; but others petitioned Christ on his behalf. It is a picture of the saints’ work on behalf of sinners. It is a great ministry to pray for the lost to be saved or to pray to God about solving other needs of folk.

Two things can be said about this petition. First, it was a passionate petition; and, second, it was a presumptive petition.

_A passionate petition_. “Beseech” informs us that “they” meant business when they petitioned Christ for help for the deaf and dumb man. They were passionate in praying. They not only “bring” the man to Christ for help, but they also “beseech” Christ to help the man. We need the _beseeching_ with the _bringing_. Therefore, besides bringing others to church to hear the Gospel, we need also to pray earnestly for those we bring to church. Bringing without beseeching can be a dry run.

_A presumptive petition_. The petitioners prescribed the method Christ was to use to heal the man. They asked “him [Christ] to put his hand upon him [the deaf and dumb man].” These petitioners are not unique. Most of us presume to tell God how to answer our prayers, too. It is commendable to pray but not when we presume to tell God how to do things. As we will note shortly, Christ took some rather unusual action in the process of healing this man of his deaf and dumb condition. He did the healing His way, not the way the petitioners asked.

This dictating how God should heal reminds us of Naaman, the great Syrian general who came to Elisha for healing from his leprosy. Naaman had his idea how Elisha should heal; and when Elisha did not prescribe the healing according to Naaman’s thoughts, Naaman said, “Behold, I thought, He will surely come out to me, and stand, and call on the name of the Lord his God, and strike his hand over the place, and recover the leper” (2 Kings 5:11). Laying a hand on the afflicted was often done by Christ (Mark 6:5; 8:23, 25; Luke 4:40; 13:13), but it was not always the method Christ used. As we noted in a previous chapter, Christ did not always do things the same way. “There was nothing stereotyped about His methods. Some were healed in a crowd, others in solitude. Others were healed by a word, or by a touch, or by spittle, or by clay. There were those healed at a distance and others when present … Because of His wisdom and omnipotence, He works in ways He deems best” (Lockyer). His different ways of working helps us to put our confidence in the Master and not in the method.

**C. THE PERFORMING OF THE MIRACLE**

The response of Christ to the petition of the petitioners for healing the deaf and dumb man was to heal the man. While the petition for healing was flawed in that it presumed to dictate the method of healing, nevertheless, Christ honored what faith they evidenced and worked a miracle of healing on the deaf and dumb man. Christ often works in spite of us. This is no excuse for our failures, but it simply exhibits the grace of God in giving favors to us.

To examine Christ’s performing of this miracle of healing of the deaf and dumb man, we will note the separation for the performance, the specifics of the performance, and the success of the performance.

**1. The Separation for the Performance**

“He [Christ] took him aside from the multitude” (v. 33) to perform the miracle of healing of this deaf and dumb man. There are times when it is best for the afflicted person to be separated from the crowd when Christ remedies their problem, for it helps them to focus better on Christ. Christ likes to get us alone with Him, for we need time alone with Christ. In the case of this deaf and dumb man, being alone with Christ meant that the first voice the man would hear when his hearing was restored was that of his Blessed Healer.

While separation is important at times, God is not in the monastery business in which monks separate themselves from the world for life in an artificial action to be closer to God. This practice often caused more evil than good. But each person does need some time alone with God. Separation is needed at times because the noise and tumult of the crowd hinders our focus on Him. Thus private prayer is urged and illustrated in the Bible. As an example, Jesus said, “When thou prayest, enter into thy closet, and when thou hast shut thy door, pray to thy Father which is in secret; and thy Father which seeth in secret shall reward thee openly” (Matthew 6:6).

**2. The Specifics of the Performance**

In healing the deaf and dumb man, Christ “put his fingers into his ears, and he spit, and touched his tongue. And looking up to heaven, he sighed, and saith unto him, Ephphatha, that is, Be opened” (vv. 33, 34). At least five different actions occurred in the healing. These actions can be stated as sticking, spitting, supplicating, sighing, and speaking. The first three actions were symbolic regarding the healing. In these actions Christ communicated to the deaf and dumb man by the only way the man could understand. He could not hear Christ speak; so, by these symbolic actions, Christ showed the man what was happening.

_Sticking_. The first action of Christ in this miracle was to “put his fingers into his [the deaf man] ears” (v. 33). “Such a symbolic action was a mode of speech to the deaf and would awaken his faith and stir up in him the lively expectation of healing” (Lockyer). Christ started with the ears because they must be healed if the tongue was to ever speak normally. One reason the man could not speak well was because he could not hear. In like manner, “Those deaf to God’s words will never be able to speak freely for Him. A dull ear makes a stammering tongue” (Smith).

_Spitting_. “He [Christ] spit, and touched his [the deaf and dumb man’s] tongue” (v. 33). Interestingly, three of the thirty-three miracles of Christ, which we are studying in our book, involve Christ spitting (see chart on page 22). Christ spit in the healing of the deaf and dumb man in our text, and Christ also spit in two miracles of healing of the blind. Medical thought in those days was that there was medicinal properties in saliva. The spitting here would emphasize symbolically to the deaf man that Christ was going to heal the speech problem as well as the deaf problem. Christ was going to, therefore, heal the man completely. Like soul salvation, Christ would do a complete job.

_Supplicating_. “And looking up to heaven” (v. 34). After sticking his fingers into the deaf and dumb man’s ears and then spitting and putting some of His saliva upon the man’s tongue, Christ then looked “up to heaven.” To the deaf man, this symbolically would point to God. It would show Christ supplicating God for Divine power to heal. And it would thus show that “every good gift is from above” (James 1:17). We need more emphasis on the fact that our blessings come from above. Humanistic thinking wants us to think that men or luck are the source of blessing. It wants to leave God out. Those who sit down to a meal without praying before they eat are saying the same thing by their actions.

_Sighing_. “He sighed” (v. 34). This action was not heard by the deaf man, of course; but the action was important for it revealed the great compassion of Christ for the sin-sick world. He is not like the other priests who “cannot be touched with the feeling of our infirmities” (Hebrews 4:15). The sigh expresses Divine sympathy, a burden for the oppressed by sin. “He sighed; not as if he found any difficulty in working this miracle, or obtaining power to do it from his Father; but thus he expressed his pity for the miseries of human life, and his sympathy with the afflicted” (Henry). Christ can feel our hurt better than anyone else. Sometimes politicians tell us they feel our hurt when in fact they have no idea how we hurt or even care how we hurt. But Christ not only feels our hurt better than anyone else, He also cares about our hurt better than anyone else.

Henry adds a note to these thoughts on sighing which we do well to ponder. “To this man, he sighed … because of the many temptations which he would be exposed to, and the sins he would be in danger of, the tongue-sins, after the restoring of his speech to him, which before he was free from. He had better be tongue-tied still, unless he have grace to keep his mouth as with a bridle [Psalm 39:1].” One can also say that the ears being healed of deafness would now make them able to hear many sinful sounds—something our age is plagued with from foul speech to jungle music.

_Speaking_. “And saith unto him, Ephphatha, that is, Be opened” (v. 34). Here is a demonstration of the power of the Word of God. “He spake, and it was done. His Word shall not return void. His Word was with power” (Smith).

The words “Be opened” applied to the ear not to the tongue as the next verse (verse 35) indicates. This is the right order as we have noted several times earlier, for the ears must be right if the tongue is to speak properly.

Mark’s recording of the Aramaic word “Ephphatha” and then translating it was a characteristic practice of Mark in the Gospel account with his name. Other examples of this literary practice of Mark include: “Boanerges, which is, The sons of thunder” (3:17); “Talitha cumi; which is, being interpreted [translated], Damsel, I say unto thee arise” (5:41); “Corban, that is to say, a gift, by whatsoever thou mightest be profited by me” (7:11); “Abba, [which means] Father” (14:36); “Golgotha, which is, being interpreted [translated], The place of a skull” (15:22); and “Eloi, Eloi, lama sabachthani? which is, being interpreted [translated], My God, my God, why hast thou forsaken me?” (15:34). We point out this habit of Mark to again show the literary excellence of Scripture.

**3. The Success of the Performance**

“And straightway his ears were opened, and the string of his tongue was loosed, and he spake plain” (v. 35). From this text we note three things about the success of the performing of this miracle by Christ. They are the promptness of the success, the process of the success, and the proof of the success.

_The promptness of the success_. “Straightway” success occurred with Christ in His healing this deaf and dumb man. Once Christ spoke, the healing occurred promptly. This speed of the healing shows the great power of Christ as it does in many of Christ’s miracles. When you have the power Christ has, you can have instant success in working miracles. Deny the power of Christ, and the speed of success will diminish to nothing. Today we look at computers in terms of speed and power. The more power the computer has the more speed it has. The speed of healing in Christ’s miracles manifests tremendous power—in fact, power that is much more than human.

This matter of Divine power and speed shows up in the first chapters of the Bible. Those who embrace evolution and say the world took millions of years to become what it is today are living in denial of the power of God. Scripture says God took six solar days in creation to form the world and make man, the beasts, and vegetation. Because unbelief denies Divine power, it requires billions of years for anything to happen. This unbelief in the power of God is also why the unbeliever has trouble with all the miracles of Christ as well as all the other miracles in the Bible. Deny Divine power and you will indeed have trouble accounting for these miracles.

_The process of the success_. “His ears were opened, and the string of his tongue was loosed.” The language is, of course, figurative in describing the healing effect of the miracle. The phrase “string of his tongue was loosed” certainly describes fittingly the work of healing a tongue-tied problem. And, as we noted earlier, it indicates that the man would have had trouble speaking even if he had not been deaf.

The sequence of healing was that the ears were first opened and then the tongue spoke. As note earlier, it teaches us that we must hear before we speak. “In the spiritual realm it is the same, for the ear must be opened to receive divine instruction before the tongue is able to speak forth God’s praise. ‘I believed, and therefore have I spoken’ [2 Corinthians 4:13; cp. Psalm 116:10]” (Lockyer).

_The proof of the success_. “He spake plain.” When the man spoke plainly, the evidence was excellent that success had occurred in both the hearing problem and the speech problem in this miracle of healing.

An important spiritual lesson is found here in “he spake plain.” It tells us that when a man has an encounter with Jesus Christ, it will affect his speech in a very positive manner. As an example, when a foul mouthed man comes to Christ, you will witness the cleaning up of his language in a very conspicuous manner. A man’s speech reveals his heart. It also reveals his relationship with God. Many who claim to be converted to Christ reveal by their evil tongue the lack of genuineness of their conversion. “Out of the same mouth proceedeth blessing and cursing … Doth a fountain send forth at the same place sweet water and bitter? Can the fig tree, my brethren, bear olive berries? either a vine, figs? so can no fountain both yield salt water and fresh” (James 3:10–12).

**D. THE PRECEPT AFTER THE MIRACLE**

“And he charged them that they should tell no man; but the more he charged them, so much the more a great deal they published it” (v. 36). Once again we have a miracle situation which includes a precept in it. This precept comes after the miracle occurred. In our last chapter we also had a command given after the miracle was performed. Some of the other commands in the miracles of Christ came before the miracle or during the working of the miracle.

To examine the precept given in this miracle, we will note the rigorousness of the precept, the responsibility in the precept, the restriction in the precept, and the rebellion to the precept.

**1. The Rigorousness of the Precept**

“He charged them” (v. 36). Here we focus on the word “charged.” The word “charged” indicates that this precept was a strong one. There was force behind the giving of this order. Jesus meant business. And the forcefulness of the precept increased the necessity of obedience. As we noted in our last chapter, the greater the forcefulness of a command, the greater the peril in disobedience; and the greater the forcefulness of the command, the greater the punishment for disobedience.

**2. The Responsibility in the Precept**

“He charged them” (v. 36). The ones responsible to obey the command were “them.” Thus, the precept was given to more than just the man who was healed. It was given to those who brought the man to Christ, too. And “them” may also include any others who were close enough to witness the healing. In our prior studies where a similar command was given, the precept was given only to the one who was healed. But this precept is plainly stated as being for a number of people. Hence, many people are going to be tested here as to the sincerity of their devotion for Christ. And as we will see, many failed. Times have not changed, for few keep Christ’s precepts.

**3. The Restriction in the Precept**

“He charged them that they should tell no man” (v. 36). The precept restricted the telling about the miracle. We note the severity of the restriction, the strangeness of the restriction, and the safety in the restriction.

_The severity of the restriction_. It was a severe restriction, for “no man” was to be told—not just a few men but “no” men. Not telling a few would be hard for anyone to do after seeing a great miracle, but not telling anyone would be doubly hard. The normal tendency of people is to run out and tell everyone they can after some unusual thing has happened. The difficulty of doing this command reminds us that many times God’s commands are difficult to do. They go cross grain with our fleshly desires and habits. But that does not change the necessity of keeping the command. God’s will is not an easy path, but it is the best path.

_The strangeness of the restriction_. Again, as we have seen in previous studies where a similar restriction was given, this precept seems strange because it does not seem to be in the spirit of the Great Commission which is to tell all men. But though the precept seems strange to us, we must remember it comes from Divine wisdom. Though God’s commands and God’s will seem strange to human reasoning, that does not negate the wisdom of God’s way. It only exposes the weakness of our finite minds to discern Divine wisdom. We must recognize that God is God, therefore, we are not going to understand everything about God. And when we do not understand why God is doing something, it gives us opportunity to trust God’s wisdom. And faith in God is necessary to please God (Hebrews 11:6).

_The safety in the restriction_. One of the reasons why these restrictions were given after the miracles had occurred was for the safety of Christ. We have noted this in previous miracles where this restriction was present, and we emphasize it here inasmuch as more than just one person was told not to tell about the miracle. Timing was important in telling about these miracles because Christ’s ministry at this time was being greatly harassed by His enemies, and every new miracle only increased the peril for Christ, a peril which would hinder Christ’s path to the cross. The enemies of Christ were tools of Satan whom he would use, if he could, to prevent Christ from getting to the cross by killing Christ before that time came. Yes, Christ could have used His supernatural power to preserve His path to the cross; and sometimes He did (such as the case recorded in Luke 4:30). But He also used human means, too; such as telling people to keep the news about some miracle quiet. So actually this restriction was not so much against the Great Commission as one would think; for it, in fact, promoted the work of the Gospel by protecting Christ so He could die on the cross for our sins.

**4. The Rebellion to the Precept**

“But the more he charged them, so much the more a great deal they published it” (v. 36). “On this, as on other occasions [see Matthew 9:31; Mark 1:44, 45], the charge is nothing regarded by those on whom it is laid” (Trench). The people simply rebelled against the precept of Christ and did the very opposite He said to do. This doing the opposite of what Christ said to do is typical of human nature. Tell folk they should do something, and they will not do it. But tell them they must not do something, and that is exactly what they will do. Children especially evidence this trait. But adults are also guilty of acting this way, especially in spiritual matters. The problem goes back a long ways, for it goes back all the way to Adam and Eve who manifested the problem in the Garden of Eden. Men go the opposite of Christ because men are more likely to go the way of the flesh instead of the way of the Spirit.

**E. THE PRAISE AFTER THE MIRACLE**

“And were beyond measure astonished, saying, He hath done all things well; he maketh both the deaf to hear, and the dumb to speak” (v. 37). Praise is shown for Christ in this text in the reaction of the people to the miracle worked and in the remark of the people about the Miracle worker.

**1. Praise in the Reaction**

The people who saw the miracle “were beyond measure astonished.” The word “astonished” is translated from a Greek word which means to be nearly overcome with amazement. Then add to that the words “beyond measure” and you have a really strong reaction of astonishment to the miracle. “The astonishment at the miracle was so great that it almost deprived them of their self-possession, and it was in superabundance, and then some on top of that [beyond measure] … [they were] completely flabbergasted” (Wuest). This reaction, of course, gave great praise to the Christ. The miracle was impressive indeed! All of Christ’s works were impressive.

**2. Praise in the Remark**

“He has done all things well.” This remark by the people was a tremendous statement of praise about Christ. In fact, it was a statement which carried a lot more honor for Christ than the people who were saying it realized. And what they said was true. Christ does indeed do “all things well.”

To further examine this great statement of praise about the Miracle Worker Jesus Christ, we note the excellence for the praise, the extent of the praise, and the effect of the praise.

_The excellence for the praise_. The word translated “well” tells us about the excellence for the praise, that is, it tells us about the excellence of the work of Christ. This Greek word is _kalos_. It is translated “well” most of the time in the KJV. It means such things as “beautifully, finely, excellently, well … rightly, so that there shall be no room for blame … nobly, commendably” (Thayer). Actually the word, even though a great one in complimenting Christ, is like all adjectives of praise for Christ in that it does not do Christ justice; for He is so great in His performance that human language cannot describe the greatness adequately.

This statement reminds of the Divine comments about the creation. In Genesis 1 we have the Divine comment, “God saw that it was good,” at least four times (Genesis 1:12, 18, 25, 31). If God says it is good, it must be extremely good! Remember that Christ, being God, is said in Scripture to have created all things. “All things were made by him; and without him was not anything made that was made” (John 1:3), and Genesis says that all that He did in the creation work was “good.” In the creation as in the miracle in our text, “He hath done all things well.”

_The extent of the praise_. “All things” are included in this praise. Everything Christ did was with excellence. We note both the rebuke in “all things” and the testing in “all things.”

First, the _rebuke_ in “all things.” Christ did nothing in a mediocre way or bad way. Whatever Christ did, He did with the greatest of excellence. This rebukes our poor performances. Too often we are slovenly, careless, and inadequate in our performance especially in spiritual matters. We do only enough to get by. If we do anything well, it is only a few things—it certainly is not everything!

Second, the _testing_ in “all things.” The praise in our text came about because Christ had made “both the deaf to hear, and the dumb to speak.” But sometimes Christ allows the hearing to become deaf, the speaking to become mute, the seeing to become blind, the walking to become lame, and the living to die. When these times happen, it really tests our faith about Christ doing all things well. It is easy to say He does all things well when we are prospering; but when problems come instead of prosperity, when sickness comes instead of health, and when trial replaces tranquility, we may not be so ready to say Christ does all things well. But He still does do all things well regardless of our situations. There are no exceptions.

John the Baptist faced this test. He had been most loyal to Jesus Christ, and he had performed his ministry with excellence. Yet, after six months of faithful preaching, he was thrown in prison and later beheaded. Where was Christ? Was He doing all things well then? Why didn’t Christ intercede and get John the Baptist out of prison? But though John suffered great injustice, Christ still did all things well. The suffering that John the Baptist experienced only greatly increased his eternal rewards.

Lazarus was another case where it was hard to say Christ doeth all things well at the time. Lazarus became very sick and his sisters, Mary and Martha, sent a message to Christ about Lazarus being sick. Christ delayed His coming until Lazarus had died. Did He do all things well then? Yes, He certainly did! For the death of Lazarus provided an opportunity for Christ to perform a greater miracle of power than had Lazarus not died, for He raised Lazarus from the dead instead of only raising him from a sick bed. And as a result of this greater miracle, “many of the Jews … believed on Jesus” (John 12:11).

Yes, Christ does all things well at all times. And the knowledge of that fact is very valuable and helpful during trial. It gives us hope when things look hopeless. It gives us reason when things look unreasonable. It keeps us going in the toughest of times. Keep this truth before you at all times, and it will help you to do things well no matter what the situation is in your life.

_The effect of the praise_. In spite of the excellence of the performance of Christ which this praise was all about, many still thought Christ did evil. Though Christ had done all things well, there were still some wicked people (particularly the scribes, Pharisees, and Herodians) who wanted people to believe that Christ was an evil-doer. But when you label the Divine Personification of Perfection an evil-doer, you are exposing your own evil spiritual condition.

There is a lesson here for God’s people. Do not expect all people to think highly of you just because you are living a holy life. You may do very well in terms of character and spirituality, but that will not make all men praise you. We have personally known of those who were saved from a profligate life, yet when salvation changed their life from waste to worth, relatives and friends turned away from the new convert as though he had some terrible disease or plague. And then, of course, we have the history of persecution in which bad people persecute good people. But Scripture explains and predicts all of this animosity for holiness when it says, “He that is upright in the way is [an] abomination to the wicked” (Proverbs 29:27).
